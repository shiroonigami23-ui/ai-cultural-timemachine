🏹 Peoples & Tribes

India's Adivasi communities and Indigenous and ancient peoples worldwide: how they live and lived, what they eat, and their culture.

31 entries in 2 groups.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Gond

The Gonds, numbering about 13 million, are among India's largest Adivasi communities, living mainly in Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Maharashtra, Telangana and Odisha. They speak Gondi, a Dravidian language. Gond kingdoms such as Garha-Mandla, Deogarh and Chanda ruled central India from the 14th to 18th centuries; Rani Durgavati of Garha died fighting Akbar's army in 1564. Their worship of Persa Pen and nature, their ghotul youth dormitories and vivid Gond painting, made famous by Jangarh Singh Shyam, remain central to their culture.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Bhil

The Bhils, about 17 million people of Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, are India's largest tribal group. Famous archers, they fought alongside Maharana Pratap at Haldighati, and the Mewar coat of arms shows a Bhil warrior. They resisted British rule in uprisings led by figures such as Tantya Bhil. They speak Bhili languages, practise Pithora painting and celebrate Bhagoria before Holi.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Santhal

The Santhals (Santals), about 7 million people in Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha, Bihar and Bangladesh, speak Santali, an Austroasiatic language written in Ol Chiki, created by Pandit Raghunath Murmu in 1925. In 1855–56 brothers Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu led the Santhal Hul against landlords, moneylenders and the British. Their faith, Sarna, worships nature spirits in sacred groves; President Droupadi Murmu is Santhal.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Munda

The Mundas of Jharkhand, Odisha and Bengal speak Mundari, an Austroasiatic language. Their khuntkatti system gave land rights to the clan that cleared forest. Birsa Munda led the Ulgulan ('great tumult') of 1899–1900 against British rule and missionaries, and the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act of 1908 followed. They follow the Sarna faith and celebrate Sarhul and Karam.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Toda

The Todas, a pastoral people of the Nilgiri Hills numbering about 1,500, herd sacred buffalo and live in barrel-shaped bamboo and thatch huts. Their dairy temples are tended by priests, and their embroidery (pukhoor) has a geographical indication tag. They practised fraternal polyandry historically.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Sentinelese

The Sentinelese of North Sentinel Island in the Andamans are among the last uncontacted peoples, numbering perhaps 50–200. They reject outsiders, often with arrows, and India prohibits approaching within 5 nautical miles to protect them from disease. They hunt, fish and gather, and their ancestors probably came from Africa over 50,000 years ago.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Jarawa

The Jarawa, about 400–500 people of the Andaman Islands, lived in isolation until 1998. They hunt pigs and turtles, fish with bows and gather honey. The Andaman Trunk Road through their forest exposed them to 'human safaris', which the Supreme Court restricted.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Great Andamanese

Once about 5,000 people in ten groups, the Great Andamanese were devastated by colonial settlement, disease and violence; about 50 remain, resettled on Strait Island. Their languages are nearly extinct; the last speaker of Bo died in 2010.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Khasi

The Khasis of Meghalaya, about 1.4 million, speak an Austroasiatic language and follow a matrilineal system in which the youngest daughter (ka khadduh) inherits property. They build living root bridges and organise in traditional states (hima) led by syiems.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Naga peoples

The Naga peoples include over 16 tribes in Nagaland, Manipur and Myanmar, speaking Tibeto-Burman languages. Once famed for headhunting, which ended in the 20th century, most are now Baptist Christians. Morungs (youth dormitories), the Hornbill Festival and distinctive shawls mark their culture.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Apatani

The Apatanis of the Ziro valley in Arunachal Pradesh practise sustainable wet-rice cultivation with fish in the paddies. Older women have facial tattoos and nose plugs. They follow the Donyi-Polo faith and celebrate the Myoko festival.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Bishnoi

The Bishnoi community of Rajasthan follows 29 principles taught by Guru Jambheshwar in 1485, including protecting trees and animals. In 1730 Amrita Devi and 362 Bishnois died hugging khejri trees to stop the Jodhpur king's men cutting them, inspiring the Chipko movement.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Siddi

The Siddis, about 50,000 people of African descent in Karnataka, Gujarat and Maharashtra, descend from Bantu people brought as soldiers, sailors and slaves. Malik Ambar, a Siddi general, ruled Ahmadnagar; the Siddis of Janjira ruled a sea fort.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Maria and Muria Gond

The Maria and Muria of Bastar, Chhattisgarh, are Gond subgroups. The Muria ghotul trained young people in music, dance and responsibilities. Bastar Dussehra lasts 75 days.

Indigenous peoples & tribes of India

Rabari

The Rabaris (Rebari) are semi-nomadic pastoralists of Kutch, Saurashtra and western Rajasthan who herd camels, sheep and goats along seasonal migration routes. Their origin legends link them to Shiva and Parvati, who created the first Rabari to tend camels. Women wear black woollen garments and are famed for mirror-work and chain-stitch embroidery, each sub-group with its own motifs. Shrinking grazing land and development have pushed many to settle.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

San (Bushmen)

The San of the Kalahari are among the oldest continuous cultures, with genetic lineages diverging over 100,000 years ago. Their click languages, persistence hunting and rock art are famous. Many have lost land.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Maasai

The Maasai, about 1.5 million people of southern Kenya and northern Tanzania, speak Maa, a Nilotic language, and traditionally live by herding cattle, which provide milk, blood and meat and measure wealth. Men pass through age-sets from warriors (moran) to elders, and the adumu jumping dance is part of ceremonies. Colonial treaties (1904, 1911) took much of their best land, and today they face pressure from national parks and farming.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Aboriginal Australians

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have lived in Australia for at least 50,000–65,000 years, forming the oldest continuous cultures on Earth. Over 250 languages were spoken before 1788. The Dreaming connects law, land, ancestors and stories, and songlines mapped routes across the continent; 'fire-stick farming' shaped landscapes. British colonisation from 1788 brought frontier massacres, disease, dispossession and, until the 1970s, the forced removal of children (the Stolen Generations). The 1992 Mabo decision recognised native title.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Inuit

The Inuit of Greenland, Canada and Alaska descend from the Thule people, who spread east from Alaska about 1,000 years ago, replacing the earlier Dorset culture. They hunted seals, walrus, caribou and bowhead whales from kayaks and umiaks, built snow houses (igloos) and sod houses, and travelled by dog sled. Their clothing of caribou skin and their tools were masterpieces of Arctic adaptation. Today Nunavut (1999) and Greenland have Inuit self-government.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Sámi

The Sámi are the Indigenous people of Sápmi, spanning northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia's Kola Peninsula. Traditionally they fished, hunted and, from the 16th century, herded reindeer on seasonal migrations. Joik is their distinctive form of singing. Assimilation policies suppressed their languages and religion, but Sámi parliaments now exist in Norway, Sweden and Finland.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Ainu

The Ainu are the Indigenous people of Hokkaido, Sakhalin and the Kuril Islands, descended in part from Jōmon people. They hunted, fished for salmon and held the Iomante bear ceremony, believing kamuy (spirits) dwell in nature. From the Meiji era, Japan banned their language and customs and took their land. In 2019 Japan legally recognised them as an Indigenous people, and the Upopoy museum opened in 2020.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Yanomami

The Yanomami, about 38,000 people, live in the Amazon rainforest on the Brazil–Venezuela border in large circular communal houses (shabono). They farm plantains and manioc in forest gardens and hunt and fish. Illegal gold miners have repeatedly invaded their land, bringing violence, mercury pollution and diseases; the 1993 Haximu massacre and a 2023 health emergency drew global attention. Davi Kopenawa is a leading spokesman.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Lakota

The Lakota, part of the Sioux (Očhéthi Šakówiŋ) nation, became mounted buffalo hunters on the Great Plains after horses arrived in the 18th century. When gold was found in the sacred Black Hills, the US broke the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty; Lakota and Cheyenne under Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse defeated Custer at the Little Bighorn (1876). The near-extermination of the buffalo and the 1890 Wounded Knee massacre ended their independence. In 1980 the US Supreme Court ruled the Black Hills had been taken illegally.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Māori

Polynesian voyagers settled Aotearoa (New Zealand) around 1250–1300 CE in waka canoes, forming iwi (tribes) with chiefs, marae meeting grounds and carved wharenui houses. They adapted Polynesian crops to the colder climate and hunted moa to extinction. The Treaty of Waitangi (1840) with Britain was followed by land wars and confiscation. Today te reo Māori is an official language, and the Waitangi Tribunal addresses treaty breaches.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Bedouin

The Bedouin are Arabic-speaking nomadic pastoralists of the deserts of Arabia, the Levant, Sinai and North Africa, herding camels, goats and sheep. Organised in tribes and clans, they value hospitality, honour and poetry; Arab culture idealised Bedouin virtues. Camels, domesticated around 3,000 years ago, made long-distance desert trade and raiding possible. Modern states have encouraged them to settle.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Mongols

The Mongols are herders of the Central Asian steppe who live in portable felt gers and move seasonally with horses, sheep, goats, yaks and camels. In 1206 Temüjin united the tribes as Genghis Khan, and his successors built the largest contiguous land empire. Mongols adopted Tibetan Buddhism in the 16th century. Today about 10 million Mongols live in Mongolia, China's Inner Mongolia and Russia.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Scythians

The Scythians were Iranian-speaking nomadic horse archers who dominated the Pontic steppe from about the 9th to the 3rd century BCE. Herodotus described their kurgan burials with sacrificed horses and their use of cannabis in steam tents. They produced superb gold animal-style art, found in tombs such as Arzhan and Tolstaya Mogila. Related Saka groups invaded India as the Indo-Scythians.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Etruscans

The Etruscans flourished in Etruria (Tuscany) from about 900 to 100 BCE, in a league of city-states such as Tarquinia, Veii and Caere. They spoke a non-Indo-European language, built painted tombs, practised divination from livers, and traded with Greeks and Phoenicians. Three of Rome's kings were Etruscan, and Romans adopted their gladiatorial games, arch and togas before absorbing them.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Neanderthals

Neanderthals were Ice Age humans who lived in Europe and western and Central Asia from about 400,000 to 40,000 years ago. They hunted large animals, cared for the injured, buried their dead, used fire and made jewellery. Their DNA lives on in most people outside Africa. See also the Human Species entry.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Denisovans

Denisovans were an Asian human group known mostly from DNA in Siberia's Denisova Cave, a Tibetan jaw and, since 2025, the Harbin 'Dragon Man' skull. They interbred with modern humans, passing genes to Melanesians, Aboriginal Australians and Tibetans. See also the Human Species entry.

Peoples of the ancient & wider world

Homo floresiensis

Homo floresiensis, the 'Hobbit', was about 1.1 m tall, lived on Flores in Indonesia until about 50,000 years ago, and made stone tools and hunted dwarf elephants. Its small body may result from island dwarfism. See also the Human Species entry.
