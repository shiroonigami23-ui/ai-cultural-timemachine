How Fire Was Tamed and Cooking Changed Us

The evidence for early fire use, how people probably learned to control and make fire, and how cooking, warmth and the hearth reshaped human bodies and societies.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 2 sources🎓 Introductory

Nobody “discovered” fire; lightning and volcanoes have always made it. What our ancestors achieved, step by step over a million years, was using, carrying, controlling and finally making it.

The steps#

From wildfire to the hearth
1. **Opportunistic use (perhaps 2–1.5 million years ago):** early *Homo* may have scavenged animals cooked in bushfires and kept smouldering branches alive. Burned bones at **Swartkrans** (South Africa) and reddened earth at **Koobi Fora** (Kenya) are debated hints. 2. **Fire in a cave (~1 million years ago):** ash and burned bone deep inside **Wonderwerk Cave** (South Africa) show *Homo erectus* bringing fire indoors. 3. **Regular hearths (~400,000–300,000 years ago):** repeated hearths at **Qesem Cave** (Israel) and **Gesher Benot Ya'aqov** (~780,000 years ago) show fire becoming part of daily life. 4. **Making fire on demand:** Neanderthals probably struck flint against pyrite to make sparks (tools with strike marks ~50,000 years ago). Later people used **fire drills, bow drills and fire ploughs**, rubbing wood to make embers.

Why fire mattered#

  • Cooking: heat breaks down starch and protein and kills parasites. Primatologist Richard Wrangham argues that cooked food let our guts shrink and our brains grow; Homo erectus already had smaller teeth and a bigger brain than earlier hominins.
  • Warmth: fire let humans survive Ice Age winters and colonise cold lands.
  • Protection: predators avoid fire.
  • Longer days: firelight extended evenings, perhaps giving time for storytelling, teaching and social bonding, the first “campfire culture”.
  • Technology: fire hardened spear tips, made birch-tar glue (~200,000 years ago), heat-treated stone for sharper blades (~70,000 years ago), and much later fired pottery (~20,000 years ago) and smelted metals.
  • Landscape management: people burned grassland to attract game and encourage useful plants, as Aboriginal Australians still do with “fire-stick farming”.

Fire in culture and belief#

Fire gods and rituals appear everywhere: Agni in the Rigveda, Prometheus in Greek myth, the sacred fires of Zoroastrian temples, Diwali’s lamps, Holika Dahan bonfires and the Olympic flame.
