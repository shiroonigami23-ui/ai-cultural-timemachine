🪔 Festivals & Celebrations

Festivals of India and the world, ancient and living: origins, how they are celebrated and the food that goes with them.

36 entries in 2 groups.

Festivals of India

Diwali

Diwali, the festival of lights, falls on the new moon of Kartika (October–November) and lasts five days, from Dhanteras to Bhai Dooj. Hindus celebrate Rama's return to Ayodhya after defeating Ravana and the worship of Lakshmi, goddess of wealth; in Bengal it is Kali Puja. Jains mark Mahavira's nirvana, and Sikhs celebrate Bandi Chhor Divas, Guru Hargobind's release from Mughal prison. Homes are cleaned and lit with diyas and rangoli, sweets are exchanged, and firecrackers are lit. The 7th-century Sanskrit play Nagananda already mentions a lamp festival.

Festivals of India

Holi

Holi marks the end of winter on the full moon of Phalguna (March). The night before, bonfires (Holika Dahan) recall the burning of the demoness Holika and the survival of the devotee Prahlada; next day people throw coloured powder (gulal) and water, remembering Krishna's play with Radha and the gopis in Braj. Social hierarchies are briefly set aside. Mathura, Vrindavan and Barsana's Lathmar Holi are famous. It is mentioned in early texts like Ratnavali (7th century).

Festivals of India

Durga Puja

Durga Puja, Bengal's greatest festival in Ashvin (September–October), celebrates the goddess Durga's victory over the buffalo demon Mahishasura. Clay idols are made by artisans of Kumartuli in Kolkata and installed in elaborate pandals; Durga is welcomed as a daughter returning home with her children. Large public 'baroari' pujas began in the 18th century, sponsored by zamindars like Raja Nabakrishna Deb. On Vijaya Dashami, idols are immersed in rivers. UNESCO listed Kolkata's Durga Puja as intangible heritage in 2021.

Festivals of India

Navratri and Dussehra

Navratri ('nine nights') in Ashvin honours the goddess in her nine forms, with fasting and devotion. In Gujarat, people dance garba and dandiya; in the south, golu doll displays are set up. The tenth day, Dussehra or Vijayadashami, celebrates Rama's victory over Ravana, with Ramlila plays and burning of Ravana effigies in north India; Mysore Dasara features a royal elephant procession begun by Vijayanagara and continued by the Wodeyars.

Festivals of India

Ganesh Chaturthi

Ganesh Chaturthi in Bhadrapada (August–September) celebrates the birth of the elephant-headed god Ganesha, remover of obstacles. Household worship was long practised; in 1893 Bal Gangadhar Tilak turned it into a public festival in Pune to unite people against colonial rule. Clay idols are worshipped for up to ten days and then immersed in water amid processions. Mumbai's Lalbaugcha Raja draws millions.

Festivals of India

Pongal

Pongal, the Tamil harvest festival in mid-January (month of Thai), lasts four days. Bhogi burns old things; on Thai Pongal, new rice is boiled with milk and jaggery until it overflows ('pongal' means boiling over), symbolising abundance; Mattu Pongal honours cattle; and Kaanum Pongal is for family visits. Jallikattu bull-taming is held in some districts. Sangam-era poems mention a Thai festival.

Festivals of India

Makar Sankranti

Makar Sankranti, around 14 January, marks the Sun's entry into Capricorn and lengthening days. It is one of few Hindu festivals following the solar calendar. People bathe in rivers (Ganga Sagar Mela), eat sesame and jaggery sweets, and fly kites in Gujarat (Uttarayan). It is called Lohri in Punjab, Magh Bihu in Assam and Pongal in Tamil Nadu.

Festivals of India

Onam

Onam, Kerala's harvest festival in Chingam (August–September), welcomes the legendary king Mahabali, under whose rule all were equal, who returns from the underworld once a year. Celebrations include pookalam flower carpets, the sadya feast of over 20 dishes on banana leaves, snake-boat races (vallam kali) and the Pulikali tiger dance. An inscription from 861 CE mentions it.

Festivals of India

Bihu (Rongali)

Rongali (Bohag) Bihu marks the Assamese New Year and spring in mid-April. Young people perform the energetic Bihu dance with dhol, pepa (buffalo horn) and gogona. Cattle are bathed on Goru Bihu, and people exchange gamosa cloths. Its roots lie in agricultural fertility festivals of the Brahmaputra valley.

Festivals of India

Baisakhi

Baisakhi (Vaisakhi), on 13–14 April, celebrates the wheat harvest in Punjab. For Sikhs it marks the founding of the Khalsa by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699 at Anandpur Sahib, when he initiated the Panj Pyare. The Jallianwala Bagh massacre happened on Baisakhi in 1919. People dance bhangra and gidda, and gurdwaras hold processions.

Festivals of India

Eid al-Fitr

Eid al-Fitr ('festival of breaking the fast') ends the month of Ramadan when the new moon is sighted. Muslims attend special prayers, give zakat al-fitr charity so the poor can celebrate, wear new clothes and share sweets like sheer khurma and seviyan. It dates from Muhammad's time in Medina (624).

Festivals of India

Eid al-Adha

Eid al-Adha, during the Hajj in the month of Dhu al-Hijjah, commemorates Ibrahim's willingness to sacrifice his son, replaced by a ram. Muslims sacrifice a goat, sheep or other animal, dividing the meat among family, friends and the poor. It is called Bakrid in India.

Festivals of India

Christmas

Christmas on 25 December celebrates Jesus's birth; the date was set in Rome by the 4th century, possibly to coincide with winter festivals like Saturnalia. Customs like Christmas trees (from Germany), carols and Santa Claus (from St Nicholas) spread in the 19th century. In India, Goa, Kerala and the northeast celebrate with midnight mass and plum cake.

Festivals of India

Guru Nanak Jayanti (Gurpurab)

Celebrated on the full moon of Kartika, it marks the birth of Guru Nanak (1469) at Talwandi (Nankana Sahib, Pakistan). Sikhs hold an Akhand Path (48-hour continuous reading of the Guru Granth Sahib), dawn processions (prabhat pheri), and langar for all.

Festivals of India

Buddha Purnima

On the full moon of Vaishakha (April–May), Buddhists commemorate the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death (parinirvana). Pilgrims gather at Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Lumbini; homes are lit, and people offer flowers and practise generosity. Sri Lanka calls it Vesak, recognised by the UN since 1999.

Festivals of India

Mahavir Jayanti

Jains celebrate the birth of Mahavira, the 24th tirthankara, in Chaitra (March–April). Idols are bathed in rituals, processions carry his image, and devotees give charity and practise non-violence. Celebrations are large at Pawapuri and Girnar.

Festivals of India

Hornbill Festival

Held 1–10 December at Kisama near Kohima since 2000, the 'festival of festivals' brings together Nagaland's tribes to display dances, crafts, food and indigenous games. It is named after the hornbill bird, important in Naga folklore.

Festivals of India

Hemis Tsechu

At Ladakh's Hemis monastery in June–July, the festival honours Guru Padmasambhava's birth with masked Cham dances by monks, symbolising good over evil. Every 12 years a giant thangka is unveiled.

Festivals of India

Pushkar Camel Fair

In Kartika (October–November) in Pushkar, Rajasthan, herders trade camels, horses and cattle, and pilgrims bathe in Pushkar Lake at the Brahma temple. It features races and competitions.

Festivals of India

Kumbh Mela

The Kumbh Mela rotates among Prayagraj, Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik, recalling the myth of drops of amrit falling there. Held every 12 years at each, it is the world's largest gathering; the 2025 Maha Kumbh at Prayagraj drew hundreds of millions. Naga sadhus lead the royal bath. Xuanzang described a similar gathering at Prayag in 644. UNESCO listed it in 2017.

Festivals of India

Chhath Puja

In Bihar, Jharkhand and eastern Uttar Pradesh, devotees worship the Sun god and Chhathi Maiya over four days in Kartika, fasting without water and offering arghya to the setting and rising sun while standing in rivers.

Festivals of India

Sarhul

Sarhul, the spring festival of the Oraon, Munda and Ho peoples in Jharkhand, worships the sal tree as nature begins anew. Village priests (pahan) offer flowers to the sacred grove, and people dance and drink handia (rice beer).

Festivals of India

Hareli

Hareli, Chhattisgarh's first festival of the farming season (July–August), honours farm tools and cattle; children walk on bamboo stilts (gedi).

Festivals of the world

Chinese New Year

The Spring Festival, starting on the new moon between late January and mid-February, is China's most important holiday. Families gather for reunion dinners, give red envelopes, set off firecrackers and perform lion dances; legends say these scare away the monster Nian. It ends with the Lantern Festival. The annual migration home is the largest human migration.

Festivals of the world

Nowruz

Nowruz ('new day'), the Persian New Year at the spring equinox, has been celebrated for over 3,000 years by Zoroastrians and others in Iran, Central Asia and the Parsi community in India. Families set a haft-sin table of seven symbolic items and jump over fires on the last Wednesday of the year.

Festivals of the world

Day of the Dead

Día de los Muertos (1–2 November), mixing Aztec traditions honouring the dead with Catholic All Saints' and All Souls' Days, welcomes the souls of loved ones with altars (ofrendas), marigolds, sugar skulls and pan de muerto.

Festivals of the world

Inti Raymi

The Inca Festival of the Sun on the June solstice at Cusco honoured the sun god Inti. Banned by the Spanish in 1572, it was revived in 1944 as a re-enactment at Sacsayhuamán.

Festivals of the world

Hanukkah

Hanukkah, the eight-day Jewish festival of lights, recalls the Maccabees' rededication of the Jerusalem Temple in 164 BCE and the miracle of oil that burned for eight days. Families light the menorah.

Festivals of the world

Passover

Passover (Pesach) commemorates the Israelites' Exodus from slavery in Egypt. Families retell the story at the Seder meal and eat matzah (unleavened bread).

Festivals of the world

Easter

Easter celebrates Jesus's resurrection on the Sunday after his crucifixion. Its date was set at the Council of Nicaea (325). Customs include services, eggs and feasts.

Festivals of the world

Carnival of Rio

Rio's Carnival before Lent features samba-school parades in the Sambadrome and street parties, rooted in Portuguese and African traditions.

Festivals of the world

Songkran

Songkran, celebrated 13–15 April, marks the traditional New Year in Thailand (and as Thingyan in Myanmar, Pi Mai in Laos, and Choul Chnam Thmey in Cambodia), when the Sun enters Aries. It began as a gentle rite of pouring scented water over Buddha images and the hands of elders for blessing. Today it is also famous for nationwide water fights. UNESCO listed it as intangible heritage in 2023.

Festivals of the world

Obon

Obon, held in July or August, is a Japanese Buddhist festival when ancestors' spirits are believed to return home. Families clean graves, light welcome fires, and hold Bon Odori dances; at the end, lanterns are floated on rivers (tōrō nagashi) to guide spirits back. It derives from the Ullambana sutra story of the monk Maudgalyayana saving his mother.

Festivals of the world

Saturnalia

Saturnalia, held around 17–23 December, honoured the Roman god Saturn and recalled a mythical golden age. Social rules were reversed: slaves could feast and speak freely, people wore informal clothes and pilleus caps, gambled, exchanged gifts like candles and figurines, and chose a mock king. Many customs influenced Christmas.

Festivals of the world

Opet Festival

Egypt's Opet Festival in Thebes, during the Nile flood in the New Kingdom, carried statues of the god Amun, his wife Mut and son Khonsu in barques along an avenue of sphinxes from Karnak to Luxor temple. It renewed the pharaoh's divine power. Ordinary people received food and beer, and could ask the god questions as he passed.

Festivals of the world

Olympic Games (ancient)

The ancient Olympic Games at Olympia, held every four years from 776 BCE in honour of Zeus, began with a short footrace (stadion) and grew to include longer races, wrestling, boxing, the pankration, the pentathlon and chariot races. A sacred truce let athletes travel safely. Winners received olive wreaths and fame at home. Married women were barred, though they could own winning chariots. The games ended around 393 CE.
