🏛 Roman aqueducts

312 BCE – 3rd century CE

Aqueducts carried water by gravity alone from springs in the hills to cities, feeding fountains, baths and toilets. Most of their length ran underground; the famous arches were only needed to cross valleys.

Built312 BCE – 3rd century CE
WhereRome and across the empire
Built byRoman engineers; Appius Claudius (first aqueduct)
MaterialsStone, brick, concrete, waterproof lime mortar (opus signinum), lead pipes
WorkforceSoldiers, slaves, contracted workers
SizeRome's 11 aqueducts were about 800 km long and delivered perhaps 1 million m³ a day
Linked toRoman Republic; Roman Empire; Pont du Gard

Overview#

Aqueducts carried water by gravity alone from springs in the hills to cities, feeding fountains, baths and toilets. Most of their length ran underground; the famous arches were only needed to cross valleys.

How it was built, step by step#

  • Surveyors used a chorobates (a 6 m levelling table with a water channel) and groma to plan a route with a gentle, constant slope — the Pont du Gard’s aqueduct drops just 12.6 m over 50 km.
  • Tunnels were dug through hills from shafts at intervals, teams meeting in the middle.
  • Valleys were crossed with arcades of stacked arches, or with inverted siphons — lead or stone pipes going down and up under pressure.
  • The channel (specus) was lined with waterproof mortar and covered against dirt and sunlight.
  • Settling tanks removed sediment; at the city, a distribution castle (castellum) split water to public fountains first, then baths, then private houses.

Clever engineering and hidden stories#

Frontinus, water commissioner in 97 CE, wrote a manual exposing illegal taps by citizens. Some aqueducts, like Segovia’s, worked until the 20th century.

Legacy#

Modern water supply systems follow the same principles; the Aqua Virgo still feeds the Trevi Fountain.
