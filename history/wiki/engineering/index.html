🏗 How It Was Built

How people actually built the great works of history, step by step: the Pyramids, the Colosseum and Pantheon, Roman aqueducts and the Great Wall; India's Mohenjo-daro drains, the Kailasa temple carved from the top down, the rust-proof Iron Pillar, stepwells and the Taj Mahal; famous bridges from the Inca grass bridge and Zhaozhou to Howrah, Pamban and the Chenab; underground and wartime engineering from the Cu Chi tunnels and Iwo Jima to Derinkuyu and the Great Escape; and everyday technology like pottery, lost-wax bronze, wootz steel and qanats. Each entry covers materials, workforce, the building steps, the clever tricks and hidden stories, and its legacy.

Ancient & classical wonders

Great Pyramid of Giza

The largest of the Giza pyramids, built as Khufu's tomb, was the tallest human-made structure for about 3,800 years. Its base is level to within about 2 cm and aligned to true north to within a fraction of a degree.

Ancient & classical wonders

Colosseum

The Flavian Amphitheatre was built on the drained lake of Nero's Golden House, paid for partly with treasure looted from the Jerusalem Temple. It hosted gladiator fights, animal hunts and executions for nearly 400 years.

Ancient & classical wonders

Pantheon dome

Nearly 1,900 years later, the Pantheon still has the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. Its 8.2 m oculus is the only source of light.

Ancient & classical wonders

Roman aqueducts

Aqueducts carried water by gravity alone from springs in the hills to cities, feeding fountains, baths and toilets. Most of their length ran underground; the famous arches were only needed to cross valleys.

Ancient & classical wonders

Roman roads

Roman roads let legions march 30 km a day and carried trade and messages across the empire; 'all roads lead to Rome'.

Ancient & classical wonders

Great Wall of China

Not one wall but many, built over 2,000 years to defend against northern nomads. The brick sections tourists see near Beijing are Ming, mostly 16th century.

Ancient & classical wonders

Stonehenge

Stonehenge's giant stones align with the midsummer sunrise and midwinter sunset. Bluestones were dragged about 225 km from the Preseli Hills in Wales.

Ancient & classical wonders

Göbekli Tepe

Built 6,000 years before Stonehenge by people who did not farm, it overturned the idea that monuments only came after agriculture.

Ancient & classical wonders

Hagia Sophia

Built in under six years after the Nika riots, it was the world's largest cathedral for nearly a thousand years.

Ancient & classical wonders

Petra

Petra, the Nabataean capital, was a rich caravan city controlling incense and spice routes between Arabia and the Mediterranean. Its most famous monuments, like Al-Khazneh ('the Treasury') and the Monastery, are carved directly into rose-red sandstone cliffs.

Ancient & classical wonders

Machu Picchu

A royal estate perched on a ridge, built without mortar, wheels or iron tools.

Ancient & classical wonders

Angkor water system

Angkor, capital of the Khmer Empire, was a 'hydraulic city' covering about 1,000 km² — perhaps the largest pre-industrial urban complex. A network of reservoirs (barays), canals and moats captured monsoon rain to support rice farming and the temples.

Indian engineering

Mohenjo-daro drainage and Great Bath

Mohenjo-daro had the world's most advanced urban sanitation of its time: nearly every house had a bathing area, many had toilets, and covered drains ran under the streets.

Indian engineering

Kailasa temple, Ellora

The largest monolithic rock-cut structure in the world, carved from the top down out of one mountain of basalt as a replica of Shiva's Mount Kailash.

Indian engineering

Brihadeeswarar temple

The Big Temple's vimana was the tallest in India; granite is not found near Thanjavur.

Indian engineering

Iron Pillar of Delhi

The pillar has resisted rust for 1,600 years.

Indian engineering

Rani ki Vav stepwell

An inverted temple descending to groundwater, with over 500 sculptures; it appears on India's ₹100 note.

Indian engineering

Chand Baori stepwell

Built by King Chanda of the Nikumbha dynasty in the 9th century, Chand Baori in Abhaneri, Rajasthan, is one of the deepest and largest stepwells in India: about 3,500 narrow steps over 13 storeys descend 30 m to the water. Its geometric pattern of steps is mesmerising.

Indian engineering

Golconda Fort acoustics

Golconda began as a Kakatiya mud fort and became the capital of the Qutb Shahi sultans (1518–1687), famous for its diamond trade — the Koh-i-Noor and Hope diamond came from nearby mines. Its acoustic design lets a hand clap at the Fateh Darwaza entrance be heard at the Bala Hisar pavilion about 1 km away.

Indian engineering

Taj Mahal

Shah Jahan built the Taj Mahal as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died in 1631. Considered the jewel of Mughal architecture, it combines Persian, Central Asian and Indian elements and changes colour with the light.

Indian engineering

Konark Sun Temple

Built around 1250 by Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, the Konark Sun Temple is shaped like the Sun god Surya's giant chariot, with 24 carved stone wheels pulled by seven horses. Its main tower, which collapsed long ago, may have been about 70 m tall.

Bridges

Q'eswachaka rope bridge

The last surviving Inca suspension bridge is rebuilt every June in three days from grass. The Inca road network once had about 200 such bridges.

Bridges

Zhaozhou Bridge

The world's oldest standing open-spandrel stone arch bridge, still in use after 1,400 years.

Bridges

Pont du Gard

The tallest Roman aqueduct bridge carried water 50 km to Nîmes.

Bridges

Iron Bridge

Built over the River Severn at Coalbrookdale, where Abraham Darby I had pioneered smelting iron with coke in 1709, the Iron Bridge (1779) was the first major bridge made of cast iron. It became a symbol of the Industrial Revolution.

Bridges

Brooklyn Bridge

The first steel-wire suspension bridge; a family tragedy and triumph.

Bridges

Forth Bridge

After the Tay Bridge collapsed in a storm in 1879, killing about 75 people on a train, Scottish engineers built the Forth Bridge to be visibly massive and safe. Opened in 1890, it was the world's longest cantilever bridge and carries trains to this day.

Bridges

Golden Gate Bridge

Built during the Great Depression across the Golden Gate strait with its strong currents and fog, the Golden Gate Bridge was the world's longest suspension span (1,280 m) when it opened in 1937. Its 'International Orange' colour makes it visible in fog.

Bridges

Howrah Bridge

Built during World War II to replace a floating pontoon bridge over the Hooghly, the Howrah Bridge (Rabindra Setu) is a cantilever bridge riveted without nuts and bolts, using steel mostly from Tata Steel. It opened in 1943 without a ceremony, fearing Japanese bombing.

Bridges

Pamban Bridge

Opened in 1914, the Pamban Bridge links Rameswaram island to mainland India across the Palk Strait. It was India's first sea bridge, with a central Scherzer rolling lift span opening for ships. A new vertical-lift bridge opened alongside it in 2025.

Bridges

Chenab Rail Bridge

On the Udhampur–Srinagar–Baramulla railway, the Chenab Bridge spans a deep gorge with a 467 m steel arch, 359 m above the river — higher than the Eiffel Tower. It opened in 2025, linking Kashmir by rail.

Bridges

Living root bridges

In the rain-soaked hills of Meghalaya, Khasi and Jaintia communities grow bridges from the roots of the rubber fig tree. Unlike timber, which rots, these living bridges grow stronger over time and can last centuries.

Bridges

Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse

Opened in July 1940, the slender first Tacoma Narrows Bridge swayed so much in wind that it was nicknamed 'Galloping Gertie'. On 7 November 1940, in a 64 km/h wind, it twisted violently and collapsed into Puget Sound — captured on film.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Cu Chi tunnels

A vast underground network let guerrillas live, hide and launch surprise attacks right next to a major US base during the Vietnam War. Today it is a museum park visited by tourists.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Viet Cong booby traps

Cheap, hidden traps such as the punji stick pit were a psychological weapon that slowed and frightened US patrols. Military studies estimate booby traps and mines caused about 11% of US deaths and 17% of wounds.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Iwo Jima tunnel fortress

Instead of defending the beaches, Kuribayashi turned the tiny volcanic island into an underground fortress, inflicting about 26,000 US casualties.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Okinawa caves and Japanese underground HQ

In the Battle of Okinawa (April–June 1945), the bloodiest of the Pacific War, Japanese forces turned Okinawa's natural limestone caves (gama) and dug tunnels into command posts, hospitals and shelters. Civilians also hid in caves, and their fate was tragic.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Derinkuyu underground city

One of about 200 underground cities in Cappadocia, used as refuges during raids. It was rediscovered in 1963 when a man knocked down a wall in his house.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Maginot Line

A chain of underground forts built to stop a German invasion — which Germany simply went around through Belgium in 1940.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

London Underground air-raid shelters

During the Blitz, Londoners defied the government and sheltered in tube stations.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

Cheyenne Mountain Complex

Built during the Cold War inside a granite mountain near Colorado Springs, the Cheyenne Mountain Complex housed NORAD, which tracks missiles and aircraft over North America. It was designed to survive a nearby nuclear blast.

Underground, bunkers & war engineering

The Great Escape tunnel 'Harry'

Prisoners dug a tunnel under a German prison camp; 76 escaped in March 1944.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Potter's wheel and kiln-fired pottery

Pottery began with hunter-gatherers: fragments from Xianrendong Cave in China are about 20,000 years old. The potter's wheel, appearing in Mesopotamia around 4000 BCE, turned pottery into a fast, specialised craft — and may have inspired the wheel for carts.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Lost-wax bronze casting

A technique for casting detailed metal figures, used for the Indus 'Dancing Girl' and Chola Nataraja bronzes, still practised at Swamimalai.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Wootz steel

Wootz was a high-carbon crucible steel produced in South India and Sri Lanka from around 300 BCE. Traded to Persia and Syria, it was forged into famous 'Damascus' swords with wavy patterns, sharp and strong.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Qanat underground canals

Qanats are gently sloping underground tunnels that carry groundwater from mountain aquifers to fields and towns using gravity alone. Developed in Iran about 3,000 years ago, they spread to North Africa, Spain, China and India.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Yakhchāl ice houses and windcatchers

In the Persian desert, people made and stored ice through scorching summers using yakhchāls — domed ice houses with thick walls of insulating mortar — and cooled homes with windcatchers (badgirs) that funnel breezes.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Archimedes screw

A helix inside a tube lifts water when turned, used for irrigation and draining mines. Traditionally attributed to Archimedes, it may have been used earlier to water the gardens of Nineveh.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Dujiangyan irrigation system

Built in 256 BCE by Li Bing, governor of the Shu region for the state of Qin, Dujiangyan controls floods and irrigates the Chengdu plain without a dam — and still works after 2,270 years.

Everyday tech, crafts & water engineering

Indian muslin weaving

Bengali weavers spun phuti karpas cotton into yarn so fine that Dhaka muslin was called 'woven air'; a long sari could pass through a ring. It was prized by Romans, Mughals and European elites.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

How castle defences worked

A castle was a machine for making a small garrison able to hold off an army. Every feature — thick walls, moats, drawbridges, towers and tiny gates — was designed to slow attackers down and make them pay for every metre.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Krak des Chevaliers

T. E. Lawrence called it 'perhaps the best preserved and most wholly admirable castle in the world'. It is the finest example of a concentric castle.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Theodosian Walls of Constantinople

For a thousand years these walls protected Constantinople against Huns, Avars, Arabs, Bulgars and Rus.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Chittorgarh Fort

India's largest fort sits on a mesa above the plain, with tanks that held water for a long siege. It was besieged by Alauddin Khalji (1303), Bahadur Shah of Gujarat (1535) and Akbar (1568).

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Kumbhalgarh Fort

Kumbhalgarh's wall is among the longest continuous walls in the world, enclosing over 360 temples.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Gwalior Fort

Babur called it 'the pearl among fortresses in India'. A small temple inside has one of the oldest written zeros (876 CE).

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Daulatabad Fort

Considered one of the world's most defensible forts.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Murud-Janjira sea fort

A sea fort the Marathas, Portuguese and British could never capture.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Himeji Castle

The 'White Heron Castle' survived WWII bombing and earthquakes.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Star forts

After cannon smashed tall castle walls, engineers built low, thick walls with angled bastions.

Castles, forts & siege warfare

Trebuchet and siege engines

Siege engines were designed to break walls.
