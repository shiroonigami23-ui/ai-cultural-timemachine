🦴 Human Species

All the members of the human family tree: the earliest upright apes, australopithecines like Lucy, robust Paranthropus, early Homo, Homo erectus, Neanderthals, Denisovans, the Flores 'hobbits' and Homo sapiens, with brain size, height, tools, firsts and key fossils.

Earliest hominins

Sahelanthropus

Known from a crushed skull nicknamed Toumaï ('hope of life'), found in 2001 in the Djurab desert of Chad, Sahelanthropus tchadensis lived about 7 million years ago, near the time humans and chimpanzees split. Its brain was chimp-sized (about 360 cm³), but its small canine teeth and a forward-placed foramen magnum (the hole where the spine meets the skull) suggest it may have walked upright. A thigh bone found nearby has fuelled debate. Its location, 2,500 km west of the Rift Valley, showed early hominins were widespread in Africa.

Earliest hominins

Orrorin

Orrorin tugenensis ('original man' in the Tugen language) is known from about 20 fossils found in Kenya's Tugen Hills in 2000, dated to about 6 million years ago. Its thigh bones have a long neck and features suggesting it walked on two legs, while its curved finger bones show it still climbed trees. Its teeth have thick enamel, more human-like than chimp-like. It lived in a mix of forest and open woodland.

Earliest hominins

Ardipithecus kadabba

Found in Ethiopia's Middle Awash and dated to 5.8–5.2 million years ago, Ardipithecus kadabba is known from teeth, jaws, hand and foot bones. A toe bone suggests upright walking. Its canine teeth were somewhat ape-like, rubbing against lower teeth to sharpen themselves. It may be the ancestor of Ardipithecus ramidus.

Earliest hominins

Ardipithecus ramidus ('Ardi')

Ardi, a partial female skeleton from Aramis in Ethiopia, about 4.4 million years old, was described in 2009 after 15 years of painstaking work. She stood about 1.2 m and weighed about 50 kg, with a pelvis adapted for upright walking but a grasping, opposable big toe for climbing. Fossil plants and animals around her show she lived in woodland, overturning the idea that bipedalism began on open savanna. Males had small canines, perhaps suggesting less aggressive competition.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Australopithecus anamensis

Living about 4.2–3.8 million years ago in Kenya and Ethiopia, A. anamensis is the earliest known Australopithecus. A nearly complete 3.8-million-year-old skull from Woranso-Mille, Ethiopia (2019) revealed its face. Its leg bones show clear bipedalism. It is probably the ancestor of A. afarensis, though the two may have overlapped.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Australopithecus afarensis ('Lucy')

Lucy, discovered in 1974 at Hadar, Ethiopia, by Donald Johanson and Tom Gray, is a 40% complete skeleton about 3.2 million years old, named after the Beatles song played in camp. Her species lived from about 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago, stood 1–1.5 m, and had a brain of about 400–500 cm³. The Laetoli footprints and the 'First Family' fossils show they walked upright and lived in groups; long arms and curved fingers show they still climbed. Cut-marked bones at Dikika suggest they may have used stone to butcher meat.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Kenyanthropus

Kenyanthropus platyops ('flat-faced man of Kenya') is known from a 3.5-million-year-old skull found in 2001 at Lomekwi, west of Lake Turkana. Its flat face and small molars distinguish it from Lucy's species, showing early hominin diversity. Some researchers think it made the 3.3-million-year-old Lomekwi stone tools found nearby.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Australopithecus africanus

The first australopithecine identified, by Raymond Dart in 1925 from the Taung Child skull in South Africa, which revealed that humanity's roots lay in Africa. Living about 3.3–2.1 million years ago, it is also known from Sterkfontein's 'Mrs Ples' and 'Little Foot', a nearly complete skeleton about 3.67 million years old. Brains were about 450 cm³. Damage to the Taung skull's eye sockets shows an eagle killed the child.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Australopithecus sediba

Discovered in 2008 by nine-year-old Matthew Berger at Malapa, South Africa, A. sediba lived about 2 million years ago. Its skeletons show a mosaic: a small brain (420 cm³) and climbing arms, but a human-like hand with a long thumb and a modern-like pelvis. Some researchers suggested it could be close to the ancestor of Homo.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Paranthropus aethiopicus

Known from the 'Black Skull' (KNM-WT 17000) found near Lake Turkana in 1985, about 2.5 million years old, P. aethiopicus had a huge sagittal crest for chewing muscles and a projecting face. It is the earliest robust australopithecine and probably the ancestor of P. boisei.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Paranthropus boisei ('Nutcracker Man')

Discovered by Mary Leakey at Olduvai Gorge in 1959, 'Nutcracker Man' lived about 2.3–1.2 million years ago in East Africa. Its huge molars and massive jaw muscles were long thought to crack nuts, but chemical studies of its teeth show it ate mostly grasses and sedges. It coexisted with early Homo for about a million years before dying out.

Australopithecines & robust hominins

Paranthropus robustus

Found at Kromdraai and Swartkrans in South Africa from 1938, P. robustus lived about 2–1 million years ago. It had a big chewing apparatus and ate tough foods. Bone tools found at Swartkrans may have been used to dig termites, and burnt bones there are among early evidence of fire.

Early Homo

Homo habilis

'Handy man', named by Louis Leakey and colleagues in 1964 from Olduvai Gorge fossils, lived about 2.4–1.4 million years ago. Its brain (about 550–700 cm³) was larger than australopithecines', and it is associated with Oldowan stone tools. Its long arms suggest it still climbed. Some scientists think its fossils include more than one species.

Early Homo

Homo rudolfensis

Known from the skull KNM-ER 1470 found near Lake Turkana (then Lake Rudolf) in 1972, about 1.9 million years old, H. rudolfensis had a larger brain (about 750 cm³) and a flat, long face. It lived alongside H. habilis and H. erectus, showing several Homo species coexisted.

Early Homo

Homo ergaster ('Turkana Boy')

The 'Turkana Boy', found at Nariokotome, Kenya, in 1984, is the most complete early human skeleton, about 1.5 million years old. He was a youth about 8–12 years old and 1.6 m tall, with long legs and modern body proportions suited to walking and running long distances in heat. Some scientists treat H. ergaster as African Homo erectus. They made Acheulean hand-axes.

Early Homo

Homo georgicus (Dmanisi)

Five skulls from Dmanisi, Georgia, about 1.8 million years old, are the earliest hominins outside Africa. Their small brains (546–775 cm³) and great variation suggest early Homo left Africa before big brains and hand-axes. One toothless individual survived years after losing teeth, perhaps with help from others.

Early Homo

Homo erectus

Homo erectus ('upright man') appeared about 1.9 million years ago in Africa and spread to Georgia, Java ('Java Man', found by Eugène Dubois in 1891), China ('Peking Man' at Zhoukoudian) and India. It had human-like body proportions, a brain of about 600–1,100 cm³, made Acheulean hand-axes and likely controlled fire. It survived in Java until about 110,000 years ago — over 1.5 million years, the longest of any human species.

Early Homo

Homo antecessor

Found at Gran Dolina in Spain's Atapuerca hills, about 850,000–800,000 years old, H. antecessor had a modern-looking face. Cut marks on its bones suggest cannibalism. Proteins from its teeth suggest it was a close relative of the common ancestor of Neanderthals, Denisovans and modern humans.

Archaic humans

Homo heidelbergensis

Named from a jaw found near Heidelberg in 1907, H. heidelbergensis lived about 700,000–200,000 years ago in Africa and Europe. With a brain of about 1,100–1,400 cm³, it made hand-axes and wooden spears (Schöningen). It is often considered the ancestor of Neanderthals in Europe and Homo sapiens in Africa. The Narmada skull from India may be related.

Archaic humans

Homo naledi

Found in 2013 deep in South Africa's Rising Star cave by Lee Berger's team (who recruited slim 'underground astronauts'), H. naledi lived about 335,000–236,000 years ago — surprisingly recent for its small brain (about 560 cm³). Its feet and hands were human-like, but shoulders were adapted to climbing. Researchers controversially argue it buried its dead and engraved cave walls.

Archaic humans

Neanderthals

Homo neanderthalensis lived across Europe and western Asia from about 400,000 to 40,000 years ago. Stocky and cold-adapted, they had brains as large as ours (about 1,500 cm³), made Mousterian tools, controlled fire, buried their dead, made jewellery from eagle talons and may have painted in Spanish caves. They interbred with Homo sapiens; most non-Africans carry 1–2% Neanderthal DNA. Svante Pääbo sequenced their genome (Nobel 2022).

Archaic humans

Denisovans

Known mostly from DNA extracted from a finger bone found in 2008 in Denisova Cave in Siberia, Denisovans were sister to Neanderthals and lived across Asia. A jaw from Tibet (Xiahe) and a molar from Laos confirm their range. Melanesians and Aboriginal Australians carry up to 4–6% Denisovan DNA, and Tibetans inherited a Denisovan gene (EPAS1) that helps at high altitude.

Archaic humans

Homo longi ('Dragon Man')

A large skull found in Harbin, China, hidden in a well in 1933 and revealed in 2021, about 146,000 years old, was named Homo longi. Its brain was large (about 1,420 cm³). In 2025 proteins from the skull identified it as a Denisovan, giving that group its first face.

Archaic humans

Homo floresiensis ('Hobbit')

Found in Liang Bua cave on Flores, Indonesia, in 2003, H. floresiensis stood about 1.1 m with a brain of about 400 cm³, yet made stone tools. It lived from about 100,000 to 50,000 years ago, and ancestors on Flores date back 700,000 years. Its small size may result from island dwarfism. It hunted pygmy elephants (Stegodon).

Archaic humans

Homo luzonensis

Teeth and bones from Callao Cave in Luzon, Philippines, described in 2019 and about 50,000–67,000 years old, belong to H. luzonensis. Small teeth and curved toe bones show yet another island-dwelling human species. How its ancestors crossed the sea is unknown.

Modern humans

Homo sapiens (early)

The oldest Homo sapiens fossils, from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco (about 315,000 years ago), Omo Kibish, Ethiopia (about 233,000 years ago) and Florisbad, South Africa, show modern faces with braincases that became rounder over time. Early sapiens made Middle Stone Age tools, used ochre, collected shellfish and made beads. Genetic studies suggest our species emerged from several connected African populations.

Modern humans

Homo sapiens (out of Africa)

Some Homo sapiens reached Arabia and the Levant earlier, but the main dispersal from Africa happened about 70,000–50,000 years ago. Within 20,000 years they reached South Asia, Southeast Asia and Australia; by 45,000 years ago Europe; and by about 20,000–15,000 years ago the Americas. They interbred with Neanderthals and Denisovans, and became the only surviving human species.
