💞 Love, Desire & Society

History rarely tells this side of the story: how desire, marriage, scandal and the control of bodies changed empires, religions, laws and economies. From the myth of 'hysteria' and the first vibrators, the castrati of Baroque opera, syphilis and the birth-control pill, to Habsburg marriages, Henry VIII's break with Rome, Rasputin and the Romanovs, Casanova, harems and court eunuchs, the Kama Sutra, the tawaifs of Lucknow and the devadasis, and the laws that punished people — from Section 377 to the Magdalene laundries — and the industries all of it created. Each entry separates evidence from popular myth.

Medicine, myths & the body

Hysteria and the vibrator

For more than 2,000 years doctors blamed a wide range of women's complaints — anxiety, fainting, irritability, sleeplessness, even too much or too little desire — on a single 'disease' called hysteria, from the Greek hystera, 'womb'. Victorian doctors diagnosed it constantly, and the electric vibrator was invented in the same era. A popular story links the two: that doctors 'treated' hysteria by bringing women to orgasm by hand, and invented the vibrator to save their tired wrists. The truth is messier and more interesting.

Medicine, myths & the body

Castrati

For about 300 years, thousands of Italian boys were castrated before puberty so that their voices would not break. The operation did not make the voice lower — it kept it high, like a boy's, but powered by an adult man's lungs and chest, giving a voice of extraordinary range, power and length of breath. The best castrati were the superstars of Baroque opera, and the Catholic Church used them in choirs because women were not allowed to sing in church.

Medicine, myths & the body

Syphilis

Syphilis exploded across Europe in 1495 among the soldiers of King Charles VIII of France besieging Naples, and within five years it had spread from Scotland to India. It was a terrifying, disfiguring disease that could destroy the nose, bones and brain, and every nation blamed another: the Italians called it the French disease, the French the Neapolitan disease, the Russians the Polish disease and the Turks the Christian disease.

Medicine, myths & the body

Contraception before the Pill

People have tried to control pregnancy for at least 4,000 years. The Egyptian Kahun papyrus (c. 1850 BCE) recommends mixtures of crocodile dung and honey; Greek and Roman women used plants, the most famous being silphium, a herb from Cyrene in Libya so valued as a contraceptive that it appeared on coins — and was harvested to extinction by the 1st century CE.

Medicine, myths & the body

The birth-control pill

The Pill was the first contraceptive a woman could take privately, reliably and independently of a man. Approved in the United States in 1960 as Enovid, it was used by millions within five years and became one of the most important inventions of the 20th century.

Medicine, myths & the body

Viagra

Viagra (sildenafil) was developed by Pfizer scientists in Sandwich, England, as a heart medicine for angina. In trials it did little for the heart, but male volunteers reported an unexpected side effect, and some reportedly refused to return their leftover pills.

Medicine, myths & the body

Kinsey Reports

Alfred Kinsey, an Indiana University zoologist who had studied gall wasps, interviewed thousands of Americans about their sex lives and published Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948) and Sexual Behavior in the Human Female (1953). Both became best-sellers and shocked the country.

Medicine, myths & the body

AIDS crisis and activism

In 1981 doctors in the USA reported rare pneumonias and cancers among young gay men. The new disease, AIDS, caused by HIV, turned out to have crossed from chimpanzees to humans in central Africa early in the 20th century, and it has since killed about 40 million people.

Power, marriage & scandal

Habsburg marriage strategy

'Let others wage war; you, happy Austria, marry' was the motto attached to the Habsburgs, a family that built one of the largest empires in history largely through carefully arranged weddings rather than conquest.

Power, marriage & scandal

Henry VIII and the break with Rome

Henry VIII's desperation for a son and his passion for Anne Boleyn led him to break England away from the Catholic Church — one of the clearest examples of love and lust changing the religion of a nation.

Power, marriage & scandal

Cleopatra, Caesar and Antony

Cleopatra VII used relationships with Rome's two most powerful men to try to save Egypt's independence. Her alliances with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony were political as much as romantic, and they shaped the end of the Roman Republic.

Power, marriage & scandal

Rasputin and the fall of the Romanovs

Grigori Rasputin, a Siberian peasant mystic, gained enormous influence over Tsar Nicholas II and Empress Alexandra because he seemed able to ease the bleeding of their only son Alexei, who had haemophilia. His scandalous reputation for drinking and seducing women helped destroy the monarchy's prestige.

Power, marriage & scandal

Casanova and the libertines

Giacomo Casanova (1725–1798) became so famous for seduction that his name is now a word. But his 3,500-page memoir, History of My Life, is also one of the best pictures of 18th-century European society.

Power, marriage & scandal

Royal mistresses

In many European courts, the king's official mistress (in France, the maîtresse-en-titre) was a political figure with her own apartments, income and influence. The most powerful, Madame de Pompadour, was effectively a minister.

Power, marriage & scandal

Harems and the zenana

The harem (Arabic, 'forbidden') or zenana in India was the private women's quarters of a household. In royal palaces it held wives, mothers, concubines, daughters, servants and children, and was guarded by eunuchs — but it was also a centre of political power.

Power, marriage & scandal

Eunuchs in imperial courts

Eunuchs — castrated men — served as officials, generals and guards in the courts of Assyria, Persia, China, Byzantium, the Islamic world and India for nearly 3,000 years. Because they could not found dynasties, rulers trusted them with power, and some became among the most powerful people of their times.

Courtesans, art & culture

Kama Sutra

The Kama Sutra, written in Sanskrit by Vatsyayana around the 3rd century CE, is famous in the West as a sex manual, but only about a fifth of it deals with sexual positions. It is really a guide to living a cultured, pleasurable life — kama, one of the four aims of Hindu life alongside dharma (duty), artha (wealth) and moksha (liberation).

Courtesans, art & culture

Erotic art of temples and Pompeii

When excavators uncovered Pompeii in the 18th century, they found erotic frescoes, statues and lamps everywhere — in homes, baths, bakeries and a brothel (the Lupanar). Embarrassed authorities locked them in a 'Secret Cabinet' in Naples, open only to 'gentlemen of mature age and respectable morals' until 2000. In India, the temples of Khajuraho and Konark carry carved couples (mithuna) among thousands of other sculptures.

Courtesans, art & culture

Tawaifs of Lucknow

Tawaifs were courtesans of North India, especially Lucknow, Delhi, Benares and Hyderabad, who were above all elite performers of music, dance and poetry. Under the Nawabs of Awadh they were tutors in manners and culture to young nobles, and some were among the wealthiest women in India.

Courtesans, art & culture

Devadasis

Devadasis ('servants of god') were girls dedicated to a temple deity, ritually married to the god and trained in dance and music. In the medieval Chola period they were respected artists, and inscriptions record their gifts to temples; the dance form Bharatanatyam developed from their art.

Courtesans, art & culture

Geisha

Geisha ('person of the arts') are Japanese entertainers trained for years in music, dance, conversation and the tea ceremony. The first geisha in the 18th century were actually men, and women soon took over the profession.

Courtesans, art & culture

Courtly love and troubadours

In 12th-century Occitania, poet-musicians called troubadours invented the idea of romantic love in European literature: a knight devoted to an unattainable noble lady, often married to someone else, serving and adoring her from afar.

Law, rights & persecution

Comstock laws

The Comstock Act of 1873 made it a federal crime in the USA to send 'obscene' material through the mail — including contraceptives, information about birth control and abortion, and medical textbooks.

Law, rights & persecution

Contagious Diseases Acts

Worried that venereal disease was weakening the army and navy, Britain passed laws letting police register any woman suspected of being a prostitute in garrison towns and force her to undergo genital examinations; women found infected could be locked in 'lock hospitals' for months. Soldiers were not examined.

Law, rights & persecution

Hijras and Section 377

Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, written by Thomas Macaulay's commission in 1860 and in force from 1862, criminalised 'carnal intercourse against the order of nature', and was used against gay men and transgender people for over 150 years.

Law, rights & persecution

Oscar Wilde and Alan Turing

Two of Britain's greatest minds were destroyed by laws against homosexuality. Playwright Oscar Wilde was sentenced to two years' hard labour in 1895, and codebreaker Alan Turing was convicted in 1952 and forced to take hormones.

Law, rights & persecution

Stonewall riots

When police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar in New York, patrons and locals fought back for several nights. The riots are seen as the start of the modern gay-rights movement.

Law, rights & persecution

Magdalene laundries

Catholic religious orders in Ireland ran institutions where 'fallen women' — unmarried mothers, girls thought too flirtatious, victims of abuse — were locked up and made to work unpaid in commercial laundries.

Law, rights & persecution

Comfort women

The Japanese military forced an estimated 50,000 to 200,000 women and girls — mainly from Korea, but also China, the Philippines, Indonesia and elsewhere — into sexual slavery in military brothels called 'comfort stations'.

Industry & revolution

Sexual revolution

In the 1960s and 70s Western attitudes to sex changed faster than at any time in history: sex before marriage became normal, divorce easier, censorship weaker and gay and women's rights movements rose.

Industry & revolution

Online dating

Matchmaking moved from families, marriage brokers and newspaper adverts to the internet with Match.com (1995), then apps like Grindr (2009) and Tinder (2012) with its swipe.
