🪷 Mughal Empire

1526–1857

Ended after the 1857 uprising.

When1526–1857
RegionSouth Asia
AreaSouth Asia
Capital(s)Agra, Fatehpur Sikri, Delhi
Known forAkbar, Taj Mahal, miniature painting, Persianate culture

Overview#

Ended after the 1857 uprising.

Government and society#

The emperor held absolute power, supported by the mansabdari system: nobles (mansabdars) held numbered ranks determining their salary and the cavalry they must supply, and were paid through revenue assignments (jagirs) that were regularly transferred to prevent local power bases. Akbar’s finance minister Raja Todar Mal measured land and fixed tax as a share of average produce (the zabt system). Akbar also abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, took Rajput nobles into high office and promoted sulh-i-kul, ‘peace with all’.

How people lived#

Mughal India produced about a quarter of the world’s manufacturing, mainly textiles. Peasants paid a third or more of their produce in revenue to mansabdars, while artisans in karkhanas produced luxury goods. Cities like Agra, Delhi and Lahore were among the world’s largest, with bazaars, caravanserais and gardens. European travellers like Bernier described both splendour and peasant poverty.

Culture, beliefs and heritage#

Mughal culture blended Persian, Central Asian and Indian traditions: miniature painting, Mughal gardens (charbagh), the Taj Mahal, Hindustani music (Tansen), Urdu poetry, and cuisine like biryani and kebabs. Akbar’s policy of sulh-i-kul promoted tolerance, while Aurangzeb was more orthodox.

Food#

Mughal kitchens blended Persian, Central Asian and Indian cooking: biryani, pulao, kebabs, kormas, naan and kulfi. Babur missed Kabul’s melons; ordinary people ate khichdi, roti and dal.

Construction and architecture#

Mughal architecture blended Persian, Timurid and Indian traditions: Humayun’s Tomb (1570) with its charbagh garden; Akbar’s red sandstone city of Fatehpur Sikri and Agra Fort; Jahangir’s gardens in Kashmir; and under Shah Jahan, the white-marble Taj Mahal (1632–1653), the Red Fort and Jama Masjid of Delhi. Pietra dura inlay, domes, arches, minarets and symmetrical gardens defined the style.

Technology and knowledge#

The Mughals used gunpowder artillery and matchlock muskets (Babur won at Panipat with Ottoman-style tactics), built a road network with sarais, minted high-quality silver rupees (a standard introduced by Sher Shah Suri), and supported astronomy and the production of world-leading textiles. The Mughal economy produced roughly a quarter of world output around 1700 by Angus Maddison’s estimates.

How it rose#

Babur, a Timurid prince from Fergana descended from both Timur and Genghis Khan, invaded India and defeated Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat (1526) and Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527). His son Humayun lost the empire to Sher Shah Suri (1540) and regained it in 1555. Akbar (r. 1556–1605) won the Second Battle of Panipat and built a stable empire through conquest, alliances and reforms.

How it declined or fell#

Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707) expanded the empire to its largest extent but spent 25 years at war in the Deccan against the Marathas, emptying the treasury, while reimposing the jizya alienated many subjects. After his death, wars of succession, rebellious governors (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad), Maratha expansion, Sikh and Jat revolts, Nadir Shah’s devastating sack of Delhi (1739) and Afghan invasions shattered central power. The East India Company reduced the emperors to pensioners, and the last emperor, Bahadur Shah Zafar, was exiled after 1857.

Legacy#

Indo-Persian architecture, Mughlai cuisine, miniature painting, Hindustani music and the Urdu language, administrative practices adopted by the British, and the idea of a pan-Indian state.
