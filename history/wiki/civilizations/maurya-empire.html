🪷 Maurya Empire

322–185 BCE

Ashoka turned to Buddhism after Kalinga (261 BCE).

When322–185 BCE
RegionSouth Asia
AreaSouth Asia
Capital(s)Pataliputra
Known forChandragupta, Chanakya's Arthashastra, Ashoka's edicts and dhamma

Overview#

Ashoka turned to Buddhism after Kalinga (261 BCE).

Government and society#

The Mauryas built one of the most centralised states of the ancient world. The emperor ruled from Pataliputra with a council of ministers (mantriparishad), with provinces under royal princes at Taxila, Ujjain, Tosali and Suvarnagiri. The Arthashastra, attributed to Chanakya (Kautilya), describes departments for revenue, mines, trade, weights, forests, elephants and a network of spies. Megasthenes, the Seleucid ambassador, reported city boards managing trade, foreigners, births and deaths, and an army of perhaps 600,000 infantry, 30,000 cavalry and 9,000 elephants (likely exaggerated). Ashoka added dhamma-mahamattas, officials to promote ethics, welfare and religious harmony.

How people lived#

Megasthenes described seven classes in Mauryan society, from philosophers and farmers to soldiers and officials, and noted that India had no slaves (though the Arthashastra mentions them). Farmers paid about a quarter of their produce; the state ran mines, forests and workshops and maintained roads with milestones and rest houses. Pataliputra was a huge city with wooden walls, 570 towers and 64 gates.

Culture, beliefs and heritage#

Ashoka’s dhamma promoted non-violence, tolerance among sects, respect for elders and kindness to animals, and he banned some animal sacrifices. Buddhism spread to Sri Lanka and Central Asia, while Jainism and Brahmanical religion also flourished. Mauryan art includes polished sandstone pillars with animal capitals and the Didarganj Yakshi.

Food#

People ate rice, wheat, barley, pulses, sugarcane, dairy and fruit; Megasthenes noted rice eaten from golden bowls. The Arthashastra set rules on storehouses, fair prices and famine relief.

Construction and architecture#

Pataliputra was protected by a timber palisade about 14 km long with 570 towers, and had a pillared palace hall at Kumrahar. Ashoka erected polished sandstone pillars topped with animal capitals, such as the Lion Capital of Sarnath (India’s national emblem), built thousands of stupas including the core of the Great Stupa at Sanchi, and cut the earliest rock-cut caves at Barabar for the Ajivika sect.

Technology and knowledge#

The Mauryas built a royal highway (Uttarapatha) from Taxila to Pataliputra and on toward Bengal, with trees, wells and rest houses that Ashoka describes in his edicts. They operated state mines, mints for punch-marked silver coins, irrigation such as the Sudarshana lake in Gujarat, shipbuilding and a polished stone-working technique (the ‘Mauryan polish’) that still gleams. Ashoka’s edicts were the first large body of written Indian texts, in Brahmi and Kharosthi scripts.

How it rose#

Chandragupta Maurya, advised by the Brahmin strategist Chanakya, overthrew the unpopular Nanda dynasty of Magadha around 322 BCE, after Alexander’s withdrawal from Punjab left a power vacuum in the northwest. In about 303 BCE he defeated or made peace with Seleucus I, gaining Afghanistan and Baluchistan in exchange for 500 war elephants. His son Bindusara expanded into the Deccan, and Ashoka conquered Kalinga (261 BCE), bringing almost the whole subcontinent except the far south under one rule.

How it declined or fell#

After Ashoka’s death in 232 BCE the empire split between successors, provinces broke away, and revenue fell. Some historians blame the costs of a vast bureaucracy and army; others cite Ashoka’s pacifism weakening the military, though this is disputed. In 185 BCE the general Pushyamitra Shunga assassinated the last Mauryan king, Brihadratha, during an army review and founded the Shunga dynasty, while Indo-Greeks invaded from the northwest.

Legacy#

The first empire to unite most of South Asia; Ashoka’s edicts on tolerance, non-violence and welfare; the spread of Buddhism to Sri Lanka, Central Asia and later East Asia; the national emblem and the Ashoka Chakra on India’s flag; and the Arthashastra’s political thought.
