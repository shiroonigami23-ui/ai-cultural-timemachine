🏺 Babylonia

c. 1894–539 BCE

Babylonian astronomers tracked planets and eclipses for centuries.

Whenc. 1894–539 BCE
RegionMiddle East
AreaMesopotamia
Capital(s)Babylon
Known forHammurabi's law code, astronomy, the Hanging Gardens (legendary)

Overview#

Babylonian astronomers tracked planets and eclipses for centuries.

Government and society#

Babylon’s kings ruled with governors, judges and scribes. Hammurabi (r. ~1792–1750 BCE) united Mesopotamia and published 282 laws on a stele, covering trade, property, family, wages and crime, with punishments that depended on social class (nobles, commoners and slaves). The Neo-Babylonian kings (626–539 BCE), notably Nebuchadnezzar II, ruled a large empire through provincial governors and deportations.

How people lived#

Babylonians lived in mud-brick houses around courtyards; merchants, scribes and craftsmen formed a middle class, and slavery existed for debt and war captives. Hammurabi’s Code regulated wages, doctors’ fees, rents, marriage and divorce, showing a complex urban economy. Women could own property, and contracts were recorded on clay.

Culture, beliefs and heritage#

Marduk, patron god of Babylon, became king of the gods in the creation epic Enuma Elish, recited at the New Year (Akitu) festival. Babylonian astronomers kept records of the planets for centuries, predicting eclipses, and developed the zodiac. Their mathematics solved quadratic equations and approximated √2.

Food#

Babylonians ate barley bread, beer, dates, lentils, onions, leeks and garlic, cooked with sesame oil; the Yale tablets record some 25 stews of lamb, pigeon and vegetables seasoned with mint and coriander. Fish and dates were cheap staples, and the Code of Hammurabi even regulated beer sellers.

Construction and architecture#

Babylon became one of the largest cities in the world, with double walls, the blue glazed-brick Ishtar Gate decorated with dragons and bulls, the Processional Way, the great ziggurat Etemenanki (probably the origin of the Tower of Babel story) and Esagila, temple of Marduk. The Hanging Gardens, one of the Seven Wonders, may have been in Babylon or, as some historians argue, in Nineveh.

Technology and knowledge#

Babylonian scholars developed advanced mathematics, including place-value notation in base 60, solutions to quadratic equations and approximations of √2 accurate to six decimal places (tablet YBC 7289). Astronomers kept nightly diaries for over 600 years and could predict lunar eclipses and planetary positions, knowledge later used by Greek and Indian astronomers. They also produced the zodiac.

How it rose#

Babylon was a minor town until the Amorite king Hammurabi conquered rival cities such as Larsa, Eshnunna and Mari in the 18th century BCE. Its central position on the Euphrates and control of trade made it the cultural capital of Mesopotamia for over a thousand years.

How it declined or fell#

The first Babylonian dynasty ended when the Hittite king Mursili I sacked the city in 1595 BCE; Kassites then ruled for four centuries. After Assyrian domination, the Neo-Babylonian Empire rose under Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar, but in 539 BCE Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon, reportedly without a battle, after the unpopular king Nabonidus alienated the priesthood of Marduk.

Legacy#

Hammurabi’s law code, Babylonian astronomy, mathematics, the 7-day week linked to planets and the zodiac influenced Greece, India and the Islamic world. Babylon became a symbol of grandeur and decadence in the Bible.
