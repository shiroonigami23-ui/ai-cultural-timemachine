🪷 Gupta Empire

c. 320–550 CE

Weakened by Huna invasions.

Whenc. 320–550 CE
RegionSouth Asia
AreaNorth India
Capital(s)Pataliputra
Known forGolden age of science and art: zero, Aryabhata, Kalidasa

Overview#

Weakened by Huna invasions.

Government and society#

The Guptas ruled the Ganga heartland directly but most of their empire through feudatories and tributary kings who acknowledged their supremacy. Kings took grand titles such as Maharajadhiraja. Land grants to Brahmins and temples, recorded on copper plates, began a process of decentralisation. Faxian, a Chinese monk who visited around 400 CE, described light taxes, few death penalties and rest houses and free hospitals in the towns.

How people lived#

Gupta India had rich towns with guilds, and a growing feudal system of land grants. Faxian (about 405) reported free hospitals, rest houses and that the ‘chandalas’ (untouchables) lived outside towns and had to announce their presence — evidence of caste discrimination. Women’s position declined, with child marriage and sati appearing in some texts.

Culture, beliefs and heritage#

Classical Hinduism developed with temple worship of Vishnu and Shiva and the Puranas. Kalidasa’s plays, the Kamasutra, the Panchatantra and the Natyashastra date from this era. Nalanda was founded, and Buddhist and Jain learning thrived.

Food#

Rice, wheat, pulses, milk products, sugar (crystallised sugar was an Indian invention) and spices; Faxian noted that many people avoided meat, onions and alcohol.

Construction and architecture#

Gupta builders made some of the earliest free-standing stone Hindu temples, such as the Dashavatara temple at Deogarh and the temple at Bhitargaon in brick, the Udayagiri caves with the great boar (Varaha) relief, Buddhist sculpture at Sarnath, and the paintings of Ajanta (under the contemporary Vakatakas). The Delhi Iron Pillar, over 7 m tall and nearly rust-free after 1,600 years, was made in this era.

Technology and knowledge#

Indian mathematics and astronomy reached a peak: Aryabhata (476–550) proposed that the Earth rotates on its axis, explained eclipses, calculated π as 3.1416 and used place value; the decimal system with zero was being developed (formalised by Brahmagupta in 628). Medicine (Sushruta and Charaka traditions), metallurgy, textile dyeing and Sanskrit grammar flourished. Nalanda university was founded under Kumaragupta I.

How it rose#

The Guptas began as a minor family in Magadha. Chandragupta I (r. ~320–335) married Kumaradevi, a princess of the powerful Licchavi clan, which raised his status. His son Samudragupta (r. ~335–375), celebrated in the Allahabad pillar inscription, conquered or subdued kings across northern India and received tribute from the south. Chandragupta II (r. ~375–415) defeated the Western Kshatrapas, gaining Gujarat’s sea trade.

How it declined or fell#

From around 460 CE the Alchon Huns (Hunas) invaded from Central Asia. Skandagupta repelled them at first, but later attacks under Toramana and Mihirakula devastated the northwest and drained the treasury. At the same time feudatories such as the Maukharis and Maitrakas became independent, trade with the Roman world declined, and the dynasty faded by around 550.

Legacy#

Called India’s ‘Classical’ or ‘Golden Age’: Kalidasa’s plays and poems, the Puranas compiled, temple architecture, decimal mathematics with zero that later spread to the Arab world and Europe, and art styles that influenced Southeast and East Asia.
