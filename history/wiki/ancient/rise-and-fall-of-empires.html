How Kings, Empires and Monarchies Rose and Fell

How chiefs became kings, why monarchy spread, the common patterns in the rise and fall of empires from Akkad to the British Empire, and how monarchy lost power.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Intermediate

From bands to states#

Anthropologists describe a rough sequence (not a fixed ladder): bands of hunter-gatherers; tribes of farming villages; chiefdoms with hereditary leaders who redistributed food; and states with kings, taxes, armies, scribes and laws. Farming surpluses, the need to manage irrigation, war between neighbours and long-distance trade all pushed some chiefdoms into kingdoms.

How monarchy came into play#

Sources of royal power
- **Divine kingship:** Egyptian pharaohs were gods; Chinese emperors held the **Mandate of Heaven**; Indian kings were consecrated in the Vedic *rajasuya* and later linked to Vishnu or Shiva; Khmer kings were *devaraja* (god-kings). - **Military power:** conquerors such as Sargon, Chandragupta, Genghis Khan and Babur founded dynasties by the sword. - **Law and order:** Hammurabi presented himself as giver of justice; Ashoka ruled by *dhamma*. - **Heredity and marriage:** dynasties held power through succession and alliances (the Habsburgs famously "married" their empire). - **Election or consent:** some kings were chosen, like Gopala of the Palas, or limited by assemblies such as the Vedic *sabha* and *samiti*, Viking *things* or the English Parliament.

Patterns in the rise of empires#

Empires usually rose with a military or technological edge (chariots, iron, cavalry, gunpowder, steamships), a strong founder and capable administrators (Chanakya, Li Si), control of trade routes (Silk Road, Indian Ocean, Atlantic) and a unifying ideology or religion.

Patterns in their fall#

The Arab historian Ibn Khaldun (1377) argued that dynasties lose their founding solidarity (asabiyyah) within about four generations. Common causes: 1. Succession crises (the Mughal wars of succession, Rome’s third-century crisis). 2. Overstretch and fiscal crisis (Spain’s debts, Rome’s debased coins). 3. Climate and disease: the 4.2-kiloyear drought (Akkad), the Late Antique Little Ice Age and plague (Byzantium), drought (Maya, Angkor), smallpox (Aztec, Inca). 4. Invaders and rivals (Huns, Mongols, Europeans). 5. Internal revolt (Yellow Turbans, Taiping, 1857). 6. New technology elsewhere (gunpowder ending nomad dominance; industrial Britain overtaking Mughal and Qing economies).

How monarchy lost power#

From the Magna Carta (1215) and the English Civil War (1642–51), through the American (1776) and French (1789) revolutions, to the collapse of the Russian, German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Qing monarchies around 1911–1922, kings lost power to parliaments and republics. In India, the ~560 princely states merged into the Republic, and their privy purses were abolished in 1971. Today about 43 countries still have a monarch, mostly as constitutional figureheads; Japan’s imperial line is the oldest continuous one.
