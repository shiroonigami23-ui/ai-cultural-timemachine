⏳ Eras & Ages

Every era from deep time and human origins through the Ice Age, farming, the Bronze and Iron Ages, the classical, medieval and early modern worlds to today: how people lived, what they ate and which species shared their world.

Deep time & human origins

Pangaea and the age of dinosaurs

From about 335 to 175 million years ago most land formed one supercontinent, Pangaea, surrounded by the Panthalassa ocean. The Mesozoic Era (252–66 million years ago) — Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous — began after the Great Dying, the worst mass extinction, and saw dinosaurs dominate land, pterosaurs the air and marine reptiles the seas. Mammals appeared about 200 million years ago but stayed small and mostly nocturnal; birds evolved from feathered dinosaurs, and flowering plants spread in the Cretaceous. India drifted north as an island, and the Deccan Traps erupted near the end.

Deep time & human origins

Age of mammals (Cenozoic)

After the Chicxulub impact 66 million years ago, mammals diversified into the niches left by dinosaurs: early whales evolved in the Tethys Sea near India and Pakistan (Pakicetus), horses, elephants and primates appeared, and grasslands spread as the climate cooled. India collided with Asia, raising the Himalaya and creating the monsoon. Apes evolved in Africa and Eurasia around 25–20 million years ago, and the human line split from chimpanzees about 7 million years ago.

Deep time & human origins

Australopithecines

Between about 4.2 and 2 million years ago, Australopithecus species such as anamensis, afarensis (Lucy) and africanus lived in eastern and southern Africa. They walked upright with an arched foot but still had long arms for climbing and ape-sized brains of 400–500 cm³. The Laetoli footprints and Lucy's skeleton (found 1974 at Hadar) are their most famous evidence; some may have used sharp stones to butcher meat. Robust Paranthropus with huge chewing teeth lived alongside early Homo.

Deep time & human origins

Lower Paleolithic

The Lower Paleolithic, from the first stone tools 3.3 million years ago to about 300,000 years ago, is the longest chapter of human history. Oldowan choppers gave way to Acheulean hand-axes around 1.76 million years ago, made by Homo erectus, who spread out of Africa to Georgia, Java, China and India (Attirampakkam, about 1.5 million years ago). Control of fire appears by about 1 million years ago (Wonderwerk Cave), and wooden spears at Schöningen show big-game hunting by 300,000 years ago.

Deep time & human origins

Middle Paleolithic

From about 300,000 to 40,000 years ago, Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia and early Homo sapiens in Africa made tools with the Levallois technique, striking prepared cores to get flakes of a planned shape. Homo sapiens appeared by 315,000 years ago (Jebel Irhoud); Denisovans lived in Asia. People buried their dead, used ochre and shell beads, made glue from birch tar, and spread Out of Africa around 70,000–50,000 years ago, interbreeding with Neanderthals and Denisovans.

Deep time & human origins

Upper Paleolithic

Between about 50,000 and 12,000 years ago, Homo sapiens spread across the globe, reaching Australia, Siberia and the Americas. Blade tools, bone needles, spear-throwers, bows and harpoons appeared, along with explosive creativity: cave art at Sulawesi, Chauvet and Lascaux, the Venus figurines, bone flutes and personal ornaments. Neanderthals disappeared around 40,000 years ago, and dogs were domesticated. In India, Bhimbetka's rock shelters contain some of the oldest art.

Deep time & human origins

Last Glacial Maximum

Around 26,000–19,000 years ago ice sheets reached their maximum. Sea level was about 120 m lower, opening land bridges like Beringia; much of northern Europe was polar desert and people retreated to southern refugia. Deserts expanded, and in India the Thar was larger and drier. Mammoths, woolly rhinos and reindeer roamed the steppe-tundra.

Deep time & human origins

Mesolithic

The Mesolithic (Middle Stone Age), from about 12,000 to the arrival of farming (varying by region), followed the Ice Age. Forests spread over Europe, and people adapted with microliths — tiny flint blades set into arrows and harpoons — fishing and canoes. In India, sites like Bagor, Langhnaj and Sarai Nahar Rai show microlithic hunters and early burials, and Bhimbetka's paintings depict hunting and dancing.

Farming & first cities

Neolithic Revolution

Beginning about 10,000 BCE in the Fertile Crescent and independently in China, New Guinea, Mesoamerica, the Andes, Africa and India, people domesticated plants and animals. Wheat, barley, lentils, sheep and goats came first in West Asia; rice and millet in China; maize in Mexico. In South Asia, Mehrgarh (from about 7000 BCE) grew barley and wheat and kept cattle. Farming fed more people, leading to villages, pottery, property and later inequality, though early farmers were often shorter and less healthy than foragers.

Farming & first cities

Chalcolithic (Copper Age)

From about 5000 to 3000 BCE (later in some regions), people used copper alongside stone. Copper smelting appeared in Serbia, Iran and Anatolia; Ötzi the Iceman (about 3300 BCE) carried a copper axe. In India, Chalcolithic cultures such as Ahar-Banas, Malwa and Jorwe flourished, and early Harappan villages grew. The wheel, plough and wool sheep spread, and the first towns appeared in Mesopotamia.

Farming & first cities

Bronze Age

Bronze — copper alloyed with tin — became widespread from about 3300 BCE in the Near East, Indus Valley and Aegean, and later in China and Europe. Cities, writing, kings, temples, armies and long-distance trade developed: Sumer, Egypt's pyramids, the Indus cities, Shang China and Minoan Crete. Tin had to be traded over long distances, linking civilisations. Horses and chariots spread around 2000 BCE.

Farming & first cities

4.2-kiloyear event

Around 2200 BCE a mega-drought lasting about two centuries affected regions from Egypt to India and China, coinciding with the end of the Akkadian Empire and Egypt's Old Kingdom and stress on the Indus cities. The International Commission on Stratigraphy uses it to mark the start of the Meghalayan Age, named after Mawmluh cave in Meghalaya, whose stalagmites record it.

Farming & first cities

Late Bronze Age collapse

Around 1200–1150 BCE, the interconnected world of the eastern Mediterranean fell apart: the Hittite Empire collapsed, Mycenaean palaces burned, Ugarit was destroyed, and Egypt was weakened by the Sea Peoples. Drought, earthquakes, famine, migrations, rebellions and the breakdown of trade networks probably combined in a 'systems collapse'. Writing disappeared in Greece for centuries.

Farming & first cities

Iron Age

Iron working, harder to master than bronze but using common ore, spread after about 1200 BCE across the Near East and Europe, and independently or earlier in India (iron at sites in the Ganga valley and south India by 1800–1500 BCE) and in Africa (Nok, Meroë). Cheap iron tools and weapons let farmers clear forests and armies equip more soldiers. In India the Iron Age saw the later Vedic period and the rise of the mahajanapadas; in Europe, Celts and the Greek city-states.

Farming & first cities

Vedic period

The Vedic period (about 1500–500 BCE) is named after the Vedas, composed in Sanskrit by Indo-Aryan-speaking people in the northwest and later the Ganga plain. Early clans herded cattle and fought over pastures; later kingdoms (janapadas) formed, the varna system developed, and the Upanishads explored philosophy. Iron, rice farming and cities emerged by its end.

Classical age

Axial Age

Between about 800 and 200 BCE, new philosophies and religions emerged almost simultaneously: the Upanishads, Buddha and Mahavira in India; Confucius and Laozi in China; Zoroaster in Persia; Hebrew prophets; and Greek philosophers from Thales to Aristotle. Karl Jaspers named it the Axial Age. These traditions questioned ritual and emphasised ethics, personal transformation and reason.

Classical age

Classical antiquity

Classical antiquity in the Mediterranean ran from about the 8th century BCE to the 5th century CE — Greece's city-states and philosophers, Alexander's Hellenistic world, and the Roman Republic and Empire. It produced democracy, theatre, history, geometry, Roman law, roads and concrete, and the spread of Christianity. It ended with the fall of the Western Roman Empire.

Classical age

Mauryan and post-Mauryan India

From 322 BCE the Mauryas united most of India; after 185 BCE the Shungas, Satavahanas, Indo-Greeks, Shakas and Kushans ruled different regions. Trade with Rome boomed, Gandhara and Mathura art flourished, Buddhism spread, and the Sangam poets wrote in Tamil.

Classical age

Han and Roman era

From about 200 BCE to 200 CE, the Han dynasty in China and the Roman Empire were the world's two great empires, each with about 60 million people. The Silk Road linked them through Parthia and the Kushans. Both built roads, standardised coins and administered vast territories, and both fell into crisis in the 3rd century.

Classical age

Classical Golden Age of India (Gupta)

From about 320 to 550 CE, the Gupta Empire under Chandragupta I, Samudragupta and Chandragupta II ruled north India from Pataliputra and Ujjain. It was a classical age of Sanskrit literature (Kalidasa's Shakuntala and Meghaduta), mathematics and astronomy (Aryabhata's place-value system and rotating Earth), the codification of Puranas and law books, early stone temples such as Deogarh, and the paintings of Ajanta under the allied Vakatakas. Nalanda was founded around 427. Huna invasions and the rise of feudatories ended Gupta power by the mid-6th century.

Medieval world

Late Antique Little Ice Age

Three major volcanic eruptions in 536, 540 and 547 CE cooled the Northern Hemisphere by up to 2 °C for over a century (until about 660), according to tree rings. Crop failures, the Plague of Justinian from 541 and migrations of steppe peoples like the Avars coincided. The Byzantine and Sasanian empires were weakened just before the Arab conquests, and the Chinese Sui and Tang dynasties rose from the turmoil.

Medieval world

Early Middle Ages

From about 500 to 1000 CE, the Western Roman Empire gave way to Germanic kingdoms — Franks, Visigoths, Anglo-Saxons — while the Byzantine Empire continued in the east. Islam rose and spread from Spain to Sindh; Tang China flourished; in India, the Chalukyas, Pallavas, Rashtrakutas, Palas and Pratiharas competed, and temple architecture blossomed. The Vikings raided Europe from 793, and Charlemagne briefly revived a western empire.

Medieval world

Islamic Golden Age

From roughly the 8th to the 13th century, scholars in Baghdad's House of Wisdom, Cairo, Córdoba, Bukhara and Samarkand translated and expanded Greek, Persian and Indian knowledge. Al-Khwarizmi founded algebra and spread Indian numerals; Ibn al-Haytham pioneered optics and experimental method; Ibn Sina wrote the Canon of Medicine; al-Biruni studied India; astronomers built observatories. Paper, learned from China, made books cheap. The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 is often seen as its end.

Medieval world

Medieval Warm Period

From about 950 to 1250, the North Atlantic and parts of the world were warmer. Vikings settled Iceland and Greenland and reached North America; Europe's population roughly doubled; vineyards grew in England. Elsewhere it brought droughts, contributing to the decline of the Ancestral Puebloans and possibly Angkor's water stress later.

Medieval world

Chola and medieval India

From the 9th to the 13th centuries, the Cholas ruled south India and Sri Lanka and raided Southeast Asia, building the Brihadeeswarar temple. The Palas in Bengal patronised Buddhist universities, Rajputs ruled the north, and temple building reached its peak at Khajuraho, Konark and Bhubaneswar. Mahmud of Ghazni's raids and the Ghurid conquest led to the Delhi Sultanate in 1206.

Medieval world

Mongol era

In the 13th and 14th centuries the Mongol Empire, founded by Genghis Khan, became the largest contiguous land empire, stretching from Korea to Hungary. Conquests killed millions, but the Pax Mongolica revived the Silk Road, allowing Marco Polo and Ibn Battuta to travel, and spreading gunpowder, printing and paper money west. The Delhi Sultanate repelled Mongol invasions. It split into the Yuan, Ilkhanate, Chagatai and Golden Horde.

Medieval world

Black Death era

The Black Death, caused by Yersinia pestis, reached Crimea in 1346 and swept through the Middle East, North Africa and Europe by 1353, killing about a third to half of Europe's people and many elsewhere. Labour shortages raised wages and weakened serfdom; peasants rebelled (England 1381); Jews were persecuted; art became preoccupied with death. Plague recurred for centuries.

Medieval world

Little Ice Age

From about 1300 to 1850, global temperatures fell slightly, with colder periods in the 1600s linked to low solar activity and volcanic eruptions. Glaciers advanced in the Alps, the Thames froze for 'frost fairs', and crop failures contributed to famines and revolts in the 17th-century 'General Crisis' — including the fall of the Ming and the Thirty Years' War. India and China suffered droughts and famines.

Medieval world

Delhi Sultanate and Vijayanagara

From 1206 to 1526, the Delhi Sultanate's Mamluk, Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi dynasties ruled north India, repelling the Mongols. In the south, Vijayanagara (1336–1646) and the Bahmani Sultanate competed. Indo-Islamic architecture, the Persian wheel, Urdu–Hindavi, and the Bhakti and Sufi movements developed.

Early modern world

Renaissance

Beginning in 14th-century Florence and spreading across Europe by the 16th, the Renaissance revived classical Greek and Roman learning. Greek scholars fleeing Constantinople, the printing press and wealthy patrons like the Medici fuelled art (Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael), literature (Petrarch, Shakespeare), and science (Copernicus, Vesalius). Humanism focused on human potential.

Early modern world

Age of Exploration

From the 15th to 17th centuries, Portuguese and Spanish sailors, followed by the Dutch, English and French, used caravels, the compass and astrolabe to reach Africa, India, the Americas and the Pacific. Vasco da Gama reached Calicut (1498), Columbus the Caribbean (1492), and Magellan's crew circled the globe (1519–22). It began colonial empires, the Columbian Exchange and the Atlantic slave trade.

Early modern world

Mughal era

From Babur's victory at Panipat in 1526 to the deposition of Bahadur Shah Zafar in 1857, the Mughals ruled much of South Asia, reaching their height under Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb. They built the Taj Mahal, Red Fort and Fatehpur Sikri, developed the mansabdari system and produced 25% of world manufacturing output. After 1707 the empire fragmented into successor states.

Early modern world

Colonial era

From the 16th to the mid-20th century, European powers colonised the Americas, much of Asia and nearly all of Africa. Colonial rule extracted resources and labour, imposed borders, spread European languages and Christianity, and caused famines and genocides, while also introducing railways and modern education. Independence movements ended most empires after 1945.

Early modern world

Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment

From Copernicus (1543) to Newton's Principia (1687), observation and mathematics transformed science; the Enlightenment of the 18th century applied reason to politics and society. Thinkers like Locke, Voltaire, Rousseau and Kant argued for rights, tolerance and limits on power, inspiring the American and French Revolutions.

Modern world

Industrial Revolution

Beginning in Britain around 1760, machines, coal and steam power transformed textiles, iron and transport, spreading to Europe, the US and Japan. Railways, factories and cities grew rapidly. Cheap British textiles undercut Indian handloom weavers, contributing to deindustrialisation. It brought great wealth but also child labour, pollution and slums.

Modern world

British Raj

From 1858, after the 1857 rebellion, to 1947, India was ruled by the British Crown through a Viceroy. Railways, telegraphs, canals and universities were built, but famines killed tens of millions and wealth drained to Britain. The national movement grew from the Congress (1885) to Gandhi's mass campaigns and independence with Partition.

Modern world

World wars era

From 1914 to 1945, two world wars killed about 100 million people. Empires collapsed, the Russian Revolution created the Soviet Union, fascism rose in Italy and Germany, the Great Depression struck, and the Holocaust murdered six million Jews. The atomic bomb ended the second war.

Modern world

Cold War

From 1947 to 1991 the US and the Soviet Union competed without direct war, through an arms race, the space race and proxy wars in Korea, Vietnam and Afghanistan. Decolonisation created new nations; India led the Non-Aligned Movement. It ended with the collapse of the USSR.

Modern world

Information Age

Since the 1970s, microchips, personal computers, the internet (1983), the World Wide Web (1991) and smartphones have transformed communication, work and politics. India became a major IT services centre, and digital payments reached hundreds of millions. Artificial intelligence is the latest phase.

Modern world

Anthropocene

Scientists proposed the Anthropocene as a new epoch defined by human impact on Earth: climate change from greenhouse gases, plastic and radioactive markers in sediments, species extinctions and altered landscapes. In 2024 geologists voted not to formally adopt it as an epoch, but the term is widely used.
