Plagues and Pandemics in History

From the Plague of Athens to COVID-19, how epidemics killed millions, toppled empires, transformed societies and drove medical science.

📅 Reviewed 2026-11-15⏱ 2 min read🔗 3 sources🎓 Intermediate

Infectious disease has killed more people than all wars combined. Most great epidemics came from animals (zoonoses) and spread along the routes of trade, war and empire.

The three plague pandemics#

The bacterium Yersinia pestis, carried by fleas on rats and other rodents, caused three great pandemics. Ancient DNA from victims confirmed it (Bos et al. 2011).

From Justinian to Bombay
1. **Plague of Justinian (541–~750 CE):** struck Constantinople in 541, killing perhaps a fifth of the city, and returned in waves for two centuries, weakening the Byzantine and Persian empires before the Arab conquests. 2. **Black Death (1346–1353):** spread from Central Asia along Mongol trade routes, reaching Crimea, then Sicily in 1347. It killed perhaps **30–60% of Europe's population**. Labour shortages raised wages and helped end serfdom in western Europe; Jews were massacred as scapegoats; art and religion became obsessed with death. Recurrent outbreaks continued for 400 years (London's Great Plague, 1665). 3. **Third pandemic (1894–1950s):** spread from Yunnan to Hong Kong and then **Bombay (1896)**, killing about **12 million in India**. Harsh British plague measures (house searches, segregation camps) provoked protest; the assassination of plague commissioner W. C. Rand in Pune (1897) by the Chapekar brothers became a nationalist landmark. **Alexandre Yersin** identified the bacterium in 1894; **Waldemar Haffkine** developed a plague vaccine in Bombay (1897).

Other killers#

  • Smallpox: killed perhaps 300 million people in the 20th century alone; it devastated the Americas after 1520. Indian and Chinese variolation came before Jenner’s vaccine (1796); the WHO declared smallpox eradicated in 1980, after India’s last case in 1975.
  • Cholera: the first pandemic began in Bengal in 1817; John Snow traced London’s 1854 outbreak to a water pump, founding epidemiology.
  • Malaria: still kills about 600,000 a year; quinine from cinchona bark was used in colonial India (tonic water with gin).
  • 1918 influenza: about 50 million deaths worldwide, with India losing perhaps 12–17 million, the most of any country.
  • HIV/AIDS (from 1981): about 40 million deaths.
  • COVID-19 (2020–2023): about 7 million reported deaths, with excess-death estimates several times higher.

What pandemics changed#

Quarantine (from Venice’s 40-day quaranta giorni), public health boards, sewage and clean water, vaccination, germ theory, and today’s genomic surveillance all grew out of epidemics.

Sources: Bos et al. 2011, Nature · WHO: Smallpox · Wikipedia: List of epidemics
