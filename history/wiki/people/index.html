👤 People of History

Over 230 people who shaped history — emperors and queens, tyrants, freedom fighters and revolutionaries, prophets and philosophers, scientists, writers from Valmiki and Homer to Shakespeare and Premchand, artists and entertainers from Tansen to Charlie Chaplin and Marilyn Monroe, and explorers — each with a full biography and links to the empires, dynasties, events and beliefs they belong to.

Rulers & conquerors

Hammurabi

Sixth king of the First Dynasty of Babylon (r. c. 1792–1750 BCE), he turned a small city-state into an empire covering most of Mesopotamia through diplomacy and war against Larsa, Eshnunna and Mari. Late in his reign he set up stone stelae carrying 282 laws, framed as given by the sun god Shamash, so that 'the strong may not oppress the weak'. His letters show a king closely involved in canals, taxes and court cases.

Rulers & conquerors

Ramesses II

The 'Great Ancestor' of later pharaohs, Ramesses II reigned for 66 years (c. 1279–1213 BCE). He fought the Hittites at Kadesh (c. 1274 BCE), proclaimed it a victory, and later signed the world's oldest surviving peace treaty with them. He built Abu Simbel, the Ramesseum and a new capital, Pi-Ramesses, and fathered over a hundred children.

Rulers & conquerors

Hatshepsut

Daughter of Thutmose I, Hatshepsut ruled first as regent for her stepson Thutmose III and then as pharaoh (c. 1479–1458 BCE), depicted with the royal false beard. Her reign was peaceful and prosperous: she sent an expedition to the land of Punt for incense and built the terraced temple at Deir el-Bahari. After her death her images were chiselled away, perhaps to secure Thutmose III's line.

Rulers & conquerors

Cyrus the Great

Founder of the Achaemenid Persian Empire (r. c. 559–530 BCE), Cyrus overthrew the Medes, conquered Lydia and took Babylon in 539 BCE almost without a fight. The Cyrus Cylinder records that he returned deported peoples and restored their temples; the Bible praises him for letting the Jews return to Jerusalem. He died campaigning in Central Asia and was buried in a simple tomb at Pasargadae.

Rulers & conquerors

Alexander the Great

King of Macedon at 20, Alexander (356–323 BCE) was taught by Aristotle and in 11 years conquered the Persian Empire, Egypt and lands to the Indus, never losing a major battle. At the Hydaspes (326 BCE) he defeated Porus, but his exhausted army refused to go beyond the Beas. He founded cities, mixed Greek and Persian elites and spread Greek culture, but died in Babylon at 32, and his empire split among his generals.

Rulers & conquerors

Chandragupta Maurya

With the guidance of his mentor Chanakya, Chandragupta (r. c. 322–297 BCE) overthrew the Nanda dynasty and founded the Maurya Empire, the first to unite most of the Indian subcontinent. He defeated Seleucus I, who ceded the northwest in exchange for 500 elephants. Jain tradition says he abdicated, became a monk under Bhadrabahu and fasted to death at Shravanabelagola.

Rulers & conquerors

Ashoka

Grandson of Chandragupta, Ashoka (r. c. 268–232 BCE) conquered Kalinga in a war that his own edict says killed 100,000 and deported 150,000. Filled with remorse, he embraced Buddhism and dhamma — non-violence, tolerance and welfare — building hospitals, wells and rest houses and sending missions abroad. His lion capital at Sarnath is India's national emblem and his wheel is on its flag.

Rulers & conquerors

Qin Shi Huang

King Ying Zheng of Qin conquered the six rival Warring States and in 221 BCE proclaimed himself First Emperor. He standardised script, coins, weights and roads, joined border walls into an early Great Wall, and ordered the burning of books and the persecution of scholars. Obsessed with immortality, he died in 210 BCE and was buried with the Terracotta Army.

Rulers & conquerors

Julius Caesar

A Roman general and politician (100–44 BCE), Caesar conquered Gaul, crossed the Rubicon in 49 BCE to start a civil war, and became dictator. He reformed the calendar (the Julian calendar) and expanded the Senate. Senators who feared he would become king stabbed him on the Ides of March, 44 BCE; his heir Octavian became Rome's first emperor.

Rulers & conquerors

Augustus

Born Octavian, Caesar's adopted heir defeated Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BCE) and in 27 BCE became Augustus, the first Roman emperor. He kept republican forms while holding real power, began 200 years of the Pax Romana, built roads and temples, and said he found Rome brick and left it marble. The month of August is named after him.

Rulers & conquerors

Cleopatra

Cleopatra VII Philopator (69–30 BCE) was the last ruler of Ptolemaic Egypt and the only member of her Macedonian Greek dynasty to learn Egyptian; Plutarch says she spoke about nine languages. Driven from power by her brother Ptolemy XIII, she allied with Julius Caesar, who restored her to the throne in 47 BCE; she bore him a son, Caesarion. After Caesar's murder she allied with Mark Antony, bearing three children and ruling the eastern Mediterranean with him. Octavian declared war on her, and after defeat at Actium (31 BCE) both she and Antony took their own lives in Alexandria in 30 BCE — tradition says by the bite of an asp — and Egypt became a Roman province. Roman propaganda painted her as a seductress, but ancient sources also show a shrewd, educated ruler who kept Egypt independent for two decades.

Rulers & conquerors

Kanishka

Kanishka I (reigned about 127–150 CE) was the greatest ruler of the Kushan Empire, descended from Yuezhi nomads who had migrated from China's frontier to Bactria. His realm stretched from Central Asia and Afghanistan to the Ganga plain, with capitals at Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura, controlling key Silk Road routes. The Rabatak inscription, found in Afghanistan in 1993, lists his conquests and shows that he used Bactrian rather than Greek on his coins. He is remembered in Buddhist tradition for convening the Fourth Buddhist Council in Kashmir and building a giant stupa at Peshawar, though his coins also show Greek, Persian and Hindu gods like Shiva, Mithra and Helios. His reign saw the Gandhara and Mathura schools of art produce some of the first images of the Buddha in human form.

Rulers & conquerors

Samudragupta

Samudragupta (reigned about 335–375 CE), son of Chandragupta I and the Licchavi princess Kumaradevi, built the Gupta Empire through conquest. The Allahabad Pillar inscription, composed by his court poet Harishena on an Ashokan pillar, lists the kings he 'violently uprooted' in the Ganga valley, the southern rulers he defeated and then reinstated as tributaries, and frontier states and forest kingdoms that paid homage. He performed the ashvamedha horse sacrifice to proclaim universal sovereignty. His gold coins show him playing the veena, and the inscription praises him as a poet and music lover. The historian Vincent Smith later called him 'the Napoleon of India'.

Rulers & conquerors

Justinian I

Justinian I (reigned 527–565), a peasant's son from the Balkans who rose through his uncle Justin I, was the last Byzantine emperor whose first language was Latin. With his wife Theodora, a former actress who became a powerful empress, he survived the Nika riots of 532, which nearly toppled him and burned much of Constantinople; Theodora reportedly declared that 'royalty is a good burial shroud'. He rebuilt the city, including Hagia Sophia (537), and his generals Belisarius and Narses reconquered North Africa from the Vandals and Italy from the Ostrogoths. His Corpus Juris Civilis codified Roman law and later shaped European legal systems. His reign was also struck by the Plague of Justinian (541), which he survived, and his wars drained the empire.

Rulers & conquerors

Harsha

Harshavardhana (reigned 606–647) of the Pushyabhuti family of Thanesar took power at 16 after his elder brother was murdered and his sister Rajyashri's husband was killed. He moved his capital to Kannauj and united much of north India. Our knowledge comes from his court poet Banabhatta's Harshacharita and the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang, who described Harsha's great assemblies at Kannauj and Prayag, where every five years he gave away his treasure to monks, Brahmins and the poor. Harsha wrote three Sanskrit plays, Nagananda, Ratnavali and Priyadarshika, and patronised Nalanda. His southward advance was halted by the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II on the Narmada, and his empire fell apart after his death without an heir.

Rulers & conquerors

Charlemagne

Charlemagne ('Charles the Great', about 747–814) became king of the Franks in 768 and spent over 30 years at war, conquering the Lombards in Italy, the Saxons (forcibly converting them, including executing 4,500 at Verden in 782), and Bavaria, and fighting in Spain, where the Battle of Roncevaux inspired the Song of Roland. On Christmas Day 800 Pope Leo III crowned him emperor in Rome. From his capital at Aachen he sponsored the Carolingian Renaissance, gathering scholars like Alcuin of York, reforming education and promoting a clear new handwriting, Carolingian minuscule, the ancestor of our lower-case letters. He exchanged embassies with the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid, who sent him an elephant. His empire split among his grandsons, forming the basis of France and Germany.

Rulers & conquerors

Rajaraja Chola I

Rajaraja I (r. 985–1014) transformed the Chola kingdom into an empire, conquering the Cheras and Pandyas, Vengi, Gangavadi, northern Sri Lanka (destroying Anuradhapura) and the Maldives with a powerful navy. He organised a detailed land survey and revenue system, and built the Brihadeeswarar temple at Thanjavur (1010), whose vimana rises about 66 m and whose walls record gifts and temple staff. His son Rajendra I extended his conquests to the Ganga and Southeast Asia.

Rulers & conquerors

Genghis Khan

Temüjin (about 1162–1227) was born on the Mongolian steppe; after his father was poisoned, his family was abandoned and he survived poverty, captivity and the kidnapping of his wife Börte. Through alliances, loyalty-based promotion and ruthless warfare he united the Mongol and Turkic tribes, and in 1206 an assembly proclaimed him Genghis Khan ('universal ruler'). He organised his army in units of tens, hundreds and thousands, created the Yassa law code, a postal relay system and a script for Mongolian, and allowed religious freedom. His campaigns conquered northern China's Jin and Xi Xia and destroyed the Khwarazmian Empire, with massacres at cities like Nishapur and Merv. He died in 1227, and his sons and grandsons built the largest contiguous land empire in history.

Rulers & conquerors

Razia Sultan

Razia (r. 1236–1240), daughter of Iltutmish, was named heir by her father ahead of her brothers. The only woman to rule the Delhi Sultanate, she discarded the veil, wore a tunic and turban, held open court and led armies. Her promotion of the Abyssinian noble Jamal-ud-Din Yaqut angered the Turkish nobles (the 'Forty'). Governor Altunia of Bathinda rebelled and captured her; she married him and tried to regain Delhi, but both were killed in 1240.

Rulers & conquerors

Mansa Musa

Mansa Musa (r. about 1312–1337) ruled the Mali Empire at its height, controlling gold fields that supplied much of the Old World's gold. His pilgrimage to Mecca in 1324, with thousands of attendants and camels carrying gold, made him famous; his spending in Cairo depressed gold prices for years. He returned with scholars and the architect Es-Saheli, building the Djinguereber mosque and making Timbuktu a centre of Islamic learning. The 1375 Catalan Atlas shows him holding a gold nugget.

Rulers & conquerors

Krishnadevaraya

Krishnadevaraya (r. 1509–1529) of the Tuluva dynasty was Vijayanagara's greatest emperor. He defeated the Bijapur Sultanate at Raichur (1520), took territories from the Gajapatis of Odisha and kept Portuguese friendship for horses. He built gopurams at Hampi and Tirupati, and his court hosted the 'Ashtadiggajas', eight great Telugu poets, as well as Tenali Rama of folk tales. He wrote Amuktamalyada, a Telugu poem on statecraft and devotion.

Rulers & conquerors

Babur

Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur (1483–1530) was descended from Timur on his father's side and Genghis Khan on his mother's. He became ruler of Fergana at 11, repeatedly won and lost Samarkand, and seized Kabul in 1504. Invited by disaffected Afghan nobles, he invaded India and defeated Ibrahim Lodi at Panipat in 1526 using matchlocks, field guns and flanking cavalry, then defeated Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527). He founded the Mughal Empire but ruled only four years. His memoir, the Baburnama, written in Chagatai Turkic, is remarkably candid, describing battles, gardens, fruits and his homesickness for Kabul, where he asked to be buried.

Rulers & conquerors

Akbar

Jalal-ud-din Muhammad Akbar (1542–1605) became emperor at 13 after his father Humayun's death, with Bairam Khan as regent; his army won the Second Battle of Panipat (1556). He expanded the empire across north and central India, Gujarat, Bengal and Kashmir, and built a system of mansabdars (ranked officials) and a land revenue system with his finance minister Raja Todar Mal. He married Rajput princesses, appointed Hindus to high office, abolished the jizya tax and pilgrim tax, and held debates among Hindus, Muslims, Jains, Zoroastrians and Jesuits in his Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri. His idea of sulh-i-kul ('peace with all') and his short-lived Din-i Ilahi reflected religious tolerance. Though illiterate, he patronised the 'nine jewels' of his court, including Tansen and Birbal, and Abul Fazl wrote his history, the Akbarnama.

Rulers & conquerors

Shah Jahan

Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658), the fifth Mughal emperor, presided over the empire's architectural golden age. He built the Taj Mahal (1632–1653) in Agra as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, who died giving birth to their 14th child; the new capital Shahjahanabad with the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in Delhi; and the jewelled Peacock Throne. Costly wars and building strained finances. His son Aurangzeb defeated his brothers and imprisoned Shah Jahan in Agra Fort, where he died in 1666 looking at the Taj.

Rulers & conquerors

Elizabeth I

Elizabeth I (1533–1603), daughter of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn, who was executed when Elizabeth was two, survived imprisonment under her Catholic half-sister Mary I to become queen in 1558. She restored a moderate Protestant Church of England and never married, cultivating the image of the 'Virgin Queen'. Her reign saw the execution of her rival Mary, Queen of Scots (1587), the defeat of the Spanish Armada (1588), Francis Drake's circumnavigation, and a cultural flowering with Shakespeare, Marlowe and Spenser. In 1600 she chartered the East India Company, beginning Britain's involvement in India. She died childless, ending the Tudor dynasty.

Rulers & conquerors

Chhatrapati Shivaji

Shivaji Bhonsle (1630–1680) grew up in Pune under his mother Jijabai and began seizing forts from the Bijapur Sultanate as a teenager. He killed the Bijapur general Afzal Khan at Pratapgad (1659), surprised Mughal governor Shaista Khan in Pune (1663), raided Surat, and escaped from Aurangzeb's house arrest in Agra (1666) reportedly hidden in fruit baskets. He was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad in 1674, set up the Ashta Pradhan council, a navy and a revenue system, and promoted Marathi and Sanskrit in administration.

Rulers & conquerors

Peter the Great

Peter I (1672–1725) became tsar as a boy and travelled incognito to the Netherlands and England (1697–98), working in shipyards. Determined to modernise Russia, he built a navy, reorganised the army, forced nobles to shave beards and wear European clothes, and created the Table of Ranks based on service. He defeated Sweden in the Great Northern War (Poltava, 1709), founded St Petersburg as a 'window on Europe', and declared Russia an empire in 1721. He was brutal: thousands of serfs died building his capital, and he had his own son tortured to death.

Rulers & conquerors

Maharaja Ranjit Singh

Ranjit Singh (1780–1839), who lost an eye to smallpox as a child, became chief of the Sukerchakia misl and captured Lahore in 1799, uniting the Sikh misls into an empire covering Punjab, Kashmir, Multan and Peshawar. He modernised his Khalsa army with European officers, ruled with ministers of all faiths, and gilded the Harmandir Sahib (Golden Temple). He owned the Koh-i-Noor diamond. After his death, intrigues weakened the state and the British annexed Punjab in 1849.

Rulers & conquerors

Tipu Sultan

Tipu Sultan (1750–1799), son of Hyder Ali, ruled Mysore from 1782. Called the 'Tiger of Mysore', he fought four wars against the East India Company, using iron-cased rockets that later inspired Britain's Congreve rockets. He introduced a new calendar, coins and silk farming, and sought alliances with Napoleon and the Ottomans. He is admired as an anti-colonial fighter but criticised for violence against Kodavas, Malabar Hindus and Christians. He died defending Srirangapatna in 1799.

Rulers & conquerors

Napoleon Bonaparte

Napoleon Bonaparte (1769–1821), a Corsican artillery officer, rose through the French Revolution's wars, seized power in a coup in 1799 and crowned himself Emperor in 1804. His victories at Austerlitz, Jena and Wagram made him master of Europe; his Napoleonic Code established legal equality and property rights still influential worldwide. His invasion of Egypt (1798) aimed to threaten British India and led to the discovery of the Rosetta Stone. The invasion of Russia (1812) destroyed his army, and after defeat at Waterloo (1815) he died in exile on St Helena.

Rulers & conquerors

Queen Victoria

Victoria (1819–1901) became Queen of the United Kingdom at 18 in 1837 and reigned for 63 years. After the 1857 rebellion, the Crown took over India, and in 1877 she became Empress of India; she learned Hindustani from her Indian attendant Abdul Karim. Her era saw industrial growth, railways, the expansion of the British Empire to a quarter of the world, but also famines in India and Ireland. Her nine children married into Europe's royal families, earning her the title 'grandmother of Europe'.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Rani Lakshmibai

Lakshmibai (about 1828–1858), born Manikarnika in Varanasi, married the Raja of Jhansi. When he died in 1853, Governor-General Dalhousie used the Doctrine of Lapse to annex Jhansi, refusing to recognise their adopted son. In 1857 she joined the rebellion, defending Jhansi against Sir Hugh Rose's siege in 1858, then escaped with her son tied to her back and joined Tatya Tope to capture Gwalior. She died fighting near Gwalior's Phool Bagh in June 1858. Rose called her 'the most dangerous of all Indian leaders'.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Mangal Pandey

Mangal Pandey (1827–1857), a sepoy of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry at Barrackpore near Calcutta, attacked British officers on 29 March 1857, angered by rumours that new Enfield rifle cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offending Hindus and Muslims. He was hanged on 8 April. His act preceded the Meerut uprising of 10 May, and he became a symbol of the 1857 rebellion; 'Pandy' became British slang for rebels.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Mahatma Gandhi

Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), born in Porbandar, Gujarat, studied law in London and went to South Africa in 1893, where being thrown off a first-class train at Pietermaritzburg started his fight against racial discrimination. There he developed satyagraha, non-violent mass resistance, and founded Tolstoy Farm. Returning to India in 1915, he led the Champaran (1917), Kheda and Ahmedabad campaigns, the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22), the Salt March (1930) and Quit India (1942), spending about six years in prison. He promoted khadi, Hindu–Muslim unity, the end of untouchability and village self-reliance. He opposed Partition and fasted to stop communal violence in Calcutta and Delhi; on 30 January 1948 he was shot by Nathuram Godse. His methods inspired Martin Luther King Jr. and Nelson Mandela.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Jawaharlal Nehru

Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964), educated at Harrow and Cambridge, became Gandhi's close ally and a Congress leader, spending about nine years in prison. As India's first Prime Minister (1947–64), he built democratic institutions, a secular constitution, five-year plans, IITs, AIIMS and the atomic energy and space programmes. His 'Tryst with Destiny' speech marked independence. He led the Non-Aligned Movement, but the 1962 war with China damaged his reputation. He wrote The Discovery of India in jail.

Freedom fighters & leaders

B. R. Ambedkar

Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956), born into the Mahar caste, considered 'untouchable', overcame discrimination to earn doctorates from Columbia and the London School of Economics. He led the Mahad satyagraha (1927) for Dalits' right to use public water, and debated Gandhi over separate electorates (Poona Pact, 1932). As chairman of the Constituent Assembly's drafting committee, he shaped India's Constitution, abolishing untouchability. As law minister he pushed the Hindu Code Bill for women's rights. In 1956 he converted to Buddhism with about 500,000 followers, founding Navayana.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Subhas Chandra Bose

Subhas Chandra Bose (1897–1945), called Netaji, resigned from the Indian Civil Service to join the freedom movement and was elected Congress president in 1938 and 1939, but clashed with Gandhi over non-violence. Escaping house arrest in 1941, he sought help from Germany and then Japan, leading the Azad Hind government and Indian National Army in Southeast Asia with the slogan 'Give me blood, and I will give you freedom'. The INA fought at Imphal and Kohima. He reportedly died in a plane crash in Taiwan in 1945; the INA trials of 1945–46 inspired mass protests.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Bhagat Singh

Bhagat Singh (1907–1931), from a Punjabi family of revolutionaries, was shaken by the Jallianwala Bagh massacre at 12. He joined the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association; to avenge Lala Lajpat Rai's death after a police beating, he and Rajguru shot police officer John Saunders in 1928. He and Batukeshwar Dutt threw non-lethal bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly in 1929 to 'make the deaf hear'. He wrote 'Why I Am an Atheist' in jail and was hanged with Rajguru and Sukhdev on 23 March 1931, aged 23.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel

Vallabhbhai Patel (1875–1950), a lawyer from Gujarat, led the Kheda (1918) and Bardoli (1928) peasant satyagrahas, earning the title 'Sardar'. As independent India's first Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister, he persuaded or pressured over 560 princely states to join India, using military action in Hyderabad (1948). He created the modern IAS. The 182-metre Statue of Unity in Gujarat honours him.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Sarojini Naidu

Sarojini Naidu (1879–1949), the 'Nightingale of India', was a poet who published The Golden Threshold and joined the freedom struggle. She became the first Indian woman president of the Congress (1925), marched in the Salt March and was jailed during Quit India. After independence she became Governor of the United Provinces, India's first woman governor. National Women's Day is celebrated on her birthday.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Birsa Munda

Birsa Munda (1875–1900), from the Munda tribe of Chota Nagpur, led the Ulgulan ('great tumult') of 1899–1900 against British rule, landlords, moneylenders and missionaries who took tribal land. He founded a religious movement, the Birsait faith, and was called 'Dharti Aba' (father of the earth). He died in Ranchi jail aged 25. His uprising led to the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908); 15 November, his birthday, is Janjatiya Gaurav Divas and Jharkhand's foundation day.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Nelson Mandela

Nelson Mandela (1918–2013), a Xhosa lawyer, co-founded the ANC Youth League and fought apartheid, South Africa's system of racial segregation. After the Sharpeville massacre (1960), he helped found the ANC's armed wing and was sentenced to life imprisonment in 1964, spending 27 years in jail, 18 of them on Robben Island. Released in 1990, he negotiated the end of apartheid with F. W. de Klerk, shared the Nobel Peace Prize (1993) and became South Africa's first Black president in 1994, promoting reconciliation. India awarded him the Bharat Ratna in 1990.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Martin Luther King Jr.

Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968), a Baptist minister from Atlanta, led the US civil rights movement. Inspired by Gandhi's satyagraha (he visited India in 1959), he led the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) after Rosa Parks' arrest, the Birmingham campaign and the 1963 March on Washington, where he gave his 'I Have a Dream' speech. His work helped pass the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965), and he won the Nobel Peace Prize in 1964. He was assassinated in Memphis in 1968.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Winston Churchill

Winston Churchill (1874–1965), a British aristocrat, soldier and journalist, served in India, Sudan and the Boer War before entering Parliament. As First Lord of the Admiralty he backed the disastrous Gallipoli campaign (1915). In the 1930s he warned against Hitler, and as Prime Minister from May 1940 he refused to negotiate with Nazi Germany, rallying Britain with speeches like 'We shall fight on the beaches'. He worked closely with Roosevelt and Stalin, won the 1953 Nobel Prize in Literature, and returned as PM in 1951–55. In India he is remembered for opposing independence, calling Gandhi a 'half-naked fakir', and for his war cabinet's role in the Bengal famine of 1943.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Franklin D. Roosevelt

Franklin Delano Roosevelt (1882–1945), the only US president elected four times, took office in 1933 at the depth of the Great Depression. His New Deal created jobs programmes, Social Security, banking reforms and huge public works like the Tennessee Valley Authority, greatly expanding government's role. Paralysed from the waist down by polio at 39, he hid his disability from the public. He led the US into World War II after Pearl Harbor (1941), shaped the Atlantic Charter with Churchill, and planned the United Nations, but died in April 1945, weeks before victory in Europe. He also ordered the internment of Japanese Americans.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Adolf Hitler

Adolf Hitler (1889–1945), born in Austria, was a failed artist and World War I corporal who led the Nazi Party. Exploiting resentment over the Treaty of Versailles and the Great Depression, he became German Chancellor in 1933 and quickly made himself dictator. His regime persecuted Jews, Roma, disabled people and political opponents, rearmed Germany, and started World War II by invading Poland in 1939. Nazi Germany murdered six million Jews in the Holocaust. Facing defeat, Hitler killed himself in his Berlin bunker on 30 April 1945. He is history's most notorious example of how democracy can be destroyed from within.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Joseph Stalin

Joseph Stalin (1878–1953), born Ioseb Jughashvili in Georgia, was a Bolshevik revolutionary who rose to rule the Soviet Union from the late 1920s until his death. His five-year plans forced rapid industrialisation, and collectivisation of agriculture caused famines, including the Holodomor in Ukraine. The Great Purge (1936–38) executed about 750,000 people, and millions passed through the Gulag. He signed a pact with Hitler in 1939 but, after Germany invaded in 1941, led the USSR to victory at enormous cost, then imposed communist rule on Eastern Europe, beginning the Cold War.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Mao Zedong

Mao Zedong (1893–1976), son of a farmer in Hunan, co-founded the Chinese Communist Party and built a peasant-based revolution. He led the Long March (1934–35), fought Japan and won the civil war, proclaiming the People's Republic of China in 1949. Land reform and campaigns against 'enemies' followed. The Great Leap Forward (1958–62) caused a famine that killed perhaps 15–45 million people, and the Cultural Revolution (1966–76) unleashed Red Guards against intellectuals and officials. He unified China and raised literacy, but at a massive human cost.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Confucius

Kong Qiu (551–479 BCE), known as Kongzi or Confucius, was a minor official in the state of Lu during the Spring and Autumn period. He taught that society is harmonious when people follow ritual (li), practise humaneness (ren), honour their parents (filial piety) and rulers govern by virtue rather than force. He travelled among states seeking a ruler to apply his ideas, without success, and taught many students who compiled his sayings in the Analects. Under the Han dynasty Confucianism became the state ideology, shaping East Asia for 2,000 years.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Laozi

Laozi ('Old Master') is the traditional author of the Daodejing, a short book of 81 poetic chapters on the Dao ('the Way'), the natural order of the universe. Legend says he was a Zhou archivist who, leaving China on a water buffalo, wrote the book for a border guard. It teaches simplicity, humility and wu wei (effortless action). Historians debate whether he was a real person. He is honoured as the founder of Taoism.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Gautama Buddha

Siddhartha Gautama was born in Lumbini (Nepal), a prince of the Shakya republic, probably in the 5th century BCE. Moved by seeing old age, sickness and death, he left home at 29, tried severe asceticism, then found the 'Middle Way' and attained enlightenment under a pipal tree at Bodh Gaya. He preached his first sermon at Sarnath on the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path, founded the sangha including nuns, and taught for 45 years under the patronage of kings like Bimbisara. He died at Kushinagar aged 80.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Mahavira

Vardhamana Mahavira (traditionally 599–527 BCE; many scholars place him in the 5th century BCE) was born near Vaishali into a kshatriya family. At 30 he renounced his home, practised austerities for 12 years and attained kevala jnana (omniscience). He taught the five vows — non-violence (ahimsa), truth, non-stealing, chastity and non-attachment — and organised monks, nuns and laity. He is the 24th tirthankara of Jainism and attained nirvana at Pawapuri.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Socrates

Socrates (about 470–399 BCE) was an Athenian stonemason's son who served as a hoplite in the Peloponnesian War. He wrote nothing; we know him through Plato and Xenophon. He questioned people about justice, courage and virtue (the Socratic method), claiming wisdom lay in knowing that he knew nothing. Accused of impiety and corrupting youth, he was sentenced to death by drinking hemlock in 399 BCE, refusing to escape.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Plato

Plato (about 428–348 BCE), Socrates's student, founded the Academy in Athens, which lasted centuries. His dialogues, such as the Republic, Symposium and Phaedo, discuss justice, love, the soul and his theory of Forms — that the physical world is a shadow of eternal ideas (the Allegory of the Cave). The Republic imagined a state ruled by philosopher-kings. Alfred North Whitehead said Western philosophy is 'a series of footnotes to Plato'.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Aristotle

Aristotle (384–322 BCE), from Stagira, studied at Plato's Academy for 20 years, tutored the young Alexander the Great, and founded the Lyceum in Athens. He wrote on logic, biology, physics, ethics, politics, poetry and rhetoric, classifying animals and developing syllogistic logic. His works were preserved by Islamic scholars and dominated European thought in the Middle Ages.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Chanakya

Chanakya, also called Kautilya or Vishnugupta, was a Brahmin scholar, traditionally from Taxila, who helped Chandragupta Maurya overthrow the Nanda dynasty around 322 BCE and served as his chief minister. He is credited with the Arthashastra, a treatise on statecraft, economics, law, espionage and war, rediscovered in 1905. His realism has led to comparisons with Machiavelli. Legends also attribute aphorisms (Chanakya Niti) to him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Panini

Panini, a grammarian from Shalatula near Gandhara (around the 5th–4th century BCE), wrote the Ashtadhyayi, about 4,000 concise rules (sutras) describing Sanskrit's sounds, word formation and syntax. His system uses meta-rules, recursion and abbreviations similar to modern formal languages; the Backus–Naur form used in computer science is sometimes called Panini–Backus form. His grammar standardised Classical Sanskrit.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Archimedes

Archimedes (about 287–212 BCE) of Syracuse in Sicily was the greatest mathematician of antiquity. He approximated π, calculated areas and volumes using methods close to integral calculus, discovered the principle of buoyancy (reportedly shouting 'Eureka!' in his bath), and explained levers ('Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth'). He designed war machines to defend Syracuse against Rome and was killed by a Roman soldier during the city's fall.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Sushruta

Sushruta, a physician traditionally said to have taught at Kashi (Varanasi) around the 6th century BCE (the text took shape over centuries), is credited with the Sushruta Samhita. It describes over 300 surgical procedures and 120 instruments, including rhinoplasty using a cheek or forehead flap, cataract surgery and treatment of fractures, and recommends practising on gourds and dead animals. He is called the 'father of surgery'.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Charaka

Charaka, said to have been a court physician of the Kushan king Kanishka, edited the Charaka Samhita, a foundational text of Ayurveda. It explains health as a balance of three doshas (vata, pitta, kapha), describes diagnosis, diet, herbal medicines and preventive care, and contains an oath for physicians. It was translated into Persian and Arabic.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Thiruvalluvar

Thiruvalluvar, a Tamil poet-philosopher of uncertain date (perhaps 1st century BCE–5th century CE), wrote the Tirukkural, 1,330 couplets in three books on virtue (aram), wealth and politics (porul) and love (inbam). Its universal ethics, free of sectarian references, made it one of the most translated Indian works. A 133-foot statue of him stands at Kanyakumari.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Aryabhata

Aryabhata (476–550 CE) wrote the Aryabhatiya at age 23 in Kusumapura (Pataliputra) during the Gupta era. He used place-value notation, approximated π as 3.1416, gave sine tables, and proposed that the Earth rotates on its axis, explaining the apparent motion of the stars. He explained solar and lunar eclipses through shadows rather than the demon Rahu. India's first satellite (1975) was named after him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Kalidasa

Kalidasa, probably of the 4th–5th century CE and linked with the Gupta court of Chandragupta II (Vikramaditya), is the greatest poet and playwright of classical Sanskrit. His play Abhijnanashakuntalam, about King Dushyanta and Shakuntala, enchanted Goethe when translated in 1789. His poems Meghaduta (a cloud carries a message to a lover) and Raghuvamsha are masterpieces of nature imagery.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Brahmagupta

Brahmagupta (598–668), from Bhinmal in Rajasthan, wrote the Brahmasphutasiddhanta (628), the first text to treat zero as a number with rules for arithmetic: a number minus itself is zero; negative times negative is positive. He solved quadratic equations and gave Brahmagupta's formula for cyclic quadrilaterals. His work, translated into Arabic in Baghdad around 771, shaped Islamic and later European mathematics.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Adi Shankaracharya

Adi Shankara (traditionally 788–820, though some date him earlier) was born at Kaladi in Kerala and became a renunciant as a boy. He wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, Bhagavad Gita and Brahma Sutras, teaching Advaita Vedanta: the self (atman) is identical with Brahman, and the world is maya. He travelled across India debating rival schools and founded four mathas at Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri and Jyotirmath.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Al-Khwarizmi

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (about 780–850), a Persian scholar at Baghdad's House of Wisdom, wrote al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wal-Muqabala, from which 'algebra' comes. His book on Indian numerals introduced decimal place-value arithmetic to the Islamic world and, when translated into Latin, to Europe; the word 'algorithm' comes from the Latin form of his name. He also worked on astronomy and geography.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Ibn Sina (Avicenna)

Ibn Sina (980–1037), born near Bukhara, memorised the Quran by 10 and was treating patients by 18. He served Samanid and Buyid rulers as physician and vizier. His Canon of Medicine, a five-volume encyclopaedia, described contagion, clinical trials of drugs and hundreds of medicines, and was a standard textbook in Europe until the 17th century. His Book of Healing covered philosophy, logic and science, integrating Aristotle with Islamic thought.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Al-Biruni

Abu Rayhan al-Biruni (973–1048), a polymath from Khwarazm, accompanied Mahmud of Ghazni to India, learned Sanskrit and wrote Kitab al-Hind (about 1030), a remarkably objective study of Indian religion, philosophy, astronomy, mathematics and customs. He calculated the Earth's radius with impressive accuracy using a mountain and the horizon, and wrote on mineralogy and pharmacology.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Bhaskara II

Bhaskaracharya (1114–1185), head of the Ujjain observatory, wrote the Siddhanta Shiromani, which includes Lilavati (arithmetic, reportedly named for his daughter), Bijaganita (algebra) and astronomy. He solved Pell's equation with the cyclic chakravala method, described ideas resembling the derivative and Rolle's theorem, and calculated the time Earth takes to orbit the Sun.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Ibn Battuta

Ibn Battuta (1304–1369), a Moroccan legal scholar, left Tangier in 1325 for Mecca and kept travelling for 29 years, covering about 120,000 km — Africa, Arabia, Anatolia, Central Asia, India, the Maldives, Southeast Asia and China. In Delhi he served as a judge under Muhammad bin Tughlaq for eight years. His Rihla describes the Mali Empire, the Delhi Sultanate and the Black Death.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Kabir

Kabir (about 1440–1518), a weaver of Varanasi raised in a Muslim family, became a key Bhakti poet-saint. His dohas (couplets), in simple Hindi, mocked caste, idol worship, ritual and religious hypocrisy of both Hindus and Muslims, teaching devotion to a formless God within. His verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib, and the Kabir Panth follows him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Guru Nanak

Guru Nanak (1469–1539), born at Talwandi (Nankana Sahib, Pakistan), had a spiritual experience at 30 and declared 'There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim'. He travelled widely (udasis) across India, Sri Lanka, Tibet and Arabia, teaching devotion to one God (Ik Onkar), honest work, sharing (vand chhakna) and equality, and founded langar. He settled at Kartarpur and was the first of the ten Sikh Gurus.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Mirabai

Mirabai (about 1498–1547), a Rajput princess of Merta married into the royal family of Mewar, devoted herself to Krishna, whom she regarded as her true husband. Defying her in-laws, she sang and danced in temples among commoners; legends say they tried to poison her. Hundreds of her bhajans in Rajasthani and Braj are still sung.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Leonardo da Vinci

Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519), born near Florence, trained in Verrocchio's workshop and worked for the Medici, Milan's Sforza dukes and the French king Francis I. He painted The Last Supper and the Mona Lisa, and filled about 7,000 surviving notebook pages, in mirror writing, with studies of anatomy (dissecting about 30 corpses), flying machines, water, geology and engineering. He embodies the 'Renaissance man'.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Nicolaus Copernicus

Nicolaus Copernicus (1473–1543), a Polish canon and astronomer, proposed in De revolutionibus (1543) that the Earth and planets revolve around the Sun, rather than everything around the Earth (the Ptolemaic model). The book was published as he lay dying. His heliocentric model began the Scientific Revolution.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Galileo Galilei

Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), an Italian mathematician, improved the telescope and in 1610 discovered Jupiter's moons, Venus's phases and mountains on the Moon, supporting Copernicus. He studied falling bodies and pendulums, founding modern physics through experiment. The Roman Inquisition tried him in 1633 and forced him to recant; he spent his last years under house arrest. The Church acknowledged its error in 1992.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Isaac Newton

Isaac Newton (1643–1727) of England developed calculus (independently of Leibniz), discovered that white light is a spectrum, and built the reflecting telescope. In Principia Mathematica (1687) he described three laws of motion and universal gravitation, explaining both falling apples and planetary orbits. He later ran the Royal Mint and was President of the Royal Society. He also spent years on alchemy and biblical prophecy.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Charles Darwin

Charles Darwin (1809–1882) sailed on HMS Beagle (1831–36), studying fossils and wildlife, including Galápagos finches and tortoises. After 20 years of research, prompted by Alfred Russel Wallace's similar ideas, he published On the Origin of Species (1859), arguing species evolve by natural selection. The Descent of Man (1871) applied this to humans, sparking debates with religious thinkers.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Raja Ram Mohan Roy

Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), a Bengali scholar fluent in Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic and English, campaigned against sati, polygamy and child marriage and for women's property rights and modern education. He founded the Brahmo Samaj (1828), a monotheistic reform movement, and newspapers. His campaign led to the abolition of sati (1829). He died in Bristol, England, while representing the Mughal emperor.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Savitribai Phule

Savitribai Phule (1831–1897), married at nine to Jyotirao Phule, who taught her to read, became India's first woman teacher. In 1848 they opened a school for girls in Pune, facing people throwing dung at her on the way. They opened schools for Dalit children, a home for widows and their children, and founded the Satyashodhak Samaj. She died nursing plague patients in 1897.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Swami Vivekananda

Narendranath Datta (1863–1902), a disciple of the mystic Ramakrishna, became Swami Vivekananda. His speech at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago in 1893, opening 'Sisters and brothers of America', introduced Vedanta and yoga to the West. He founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897) for social service and inspired Indian nationalism. National Youth Day is on his birthday.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Rabindranath Tagore

Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), Bengali poet, novelist, composer and painter, became the first non-European Nobel laureate in Literature (1913) for Gitanjali. He wrote the national anthems of India (Jana Gana Mana) and Bangladesh (Amar Shonar Bangla), founded Visva-Bharati University at Santiniketan, and returned his knighthood after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre. He called Gandhi 'Mahatma'.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Marie Curie

Marie Skłodowska Curie (1867–1934), born in Warsaw, studied in Paris, where women could attend university. With Pierre Curie she discovered polonium (named after Poland) and radium, and coined 'radioactivity'. She won the Nobel Prize in Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911), the first person to win two. In World War I she developed mobile X-ray units. She died of radiation-induced illness.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Albert Einstein

Albert Einstein (1879–1955), working in a Swiss patent office, published four groundbreaking papers in 1905, including special relativity and E=mc². His general relativity (1915) described gravity as the curvature of spacetime. He won the 1921 Nobel Prize for the photoelectric effect. A Jew, he fled Nazi Germany in 1933 to the US, and his 1939 letter to Roosevelt led to the Manhattan Project, which he later regretted. He corresponded with Tagore and admired Gandhi.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

C. V. Raman

Chandrasekhara Venkata Raman (1888–1970) discovered in 1928 in Calcutta that light changes wavelength when scattered by molecules — the Raman effect — winning the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics, the first Asian science Nobel. He founded the Raman Research Institute in Bangalore. India celebrates National Science Day on 28 February, the day of his discovery.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Srinivasa Ramanujan

Srinivasa Ramanujan (1887–1920), a largely self-taught mathematician from Kumbakonam, sent a letter of theorems to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge in 1913. Hardy brought him to England, where he produced remarkable results on partitions, infinite series, mock theta functions and the number 1729. He became a Fellow of the Royal Society but fell ill and died in India at 32. His notebooks still inspire research.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

A. P. J. Abdul Kalam

Avul Pakir Jainulabdeen Abdul Kalam (1931–2015), from Rameswaram, sold newspapers as a boy and became an aerospace scientist. He led the development of India's first satellite launch vehicle (SLV-3, 1980) and the Agni and Prithvi missiles, earning the title 'Missile Man', and played a key role in the 1998 Pokhran nuclear tests. As President (2002–07) he was called the 'People's President' and inspired students.

Rulers & conquerors

Sargon of Akkad

Sargon (reigned about 2334–2279 BCE) founded the Akkadian Empire, often called the world's first empire. A later legend says he was born to a priestess who set him adrift in a reed basket on the Euphrates, and he was raised by a gardener; he rose to be cupbearer to King Ur-Zababa of Kish. He seized power, built a new capital at Akkad (whose location is still unknown), and defeated Lugalzagesi of Uruk to conquer all the Sumerian cities. He installed Akkadian governors, a standing army of 5,400 men, and trade links reaching Meluhha (the Indus Valley). He made his daughter Enheduanna high priestess at Ur, and his dynasty ruled for about 150 years.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Enheduanna

Enheduanna (about 2285–2250 BCE), daughter of Sargon of Akkad, was appointed high priestess of the moon god Nanna at Ur, uniting Akkadian and Sumerian religion. She composed hymns, including 'The Exaltation of Inanna' and 42 temple hymns, and is the earliest known author identified by name. She was briefly exiled during a revolt but restored.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Imhotep

Imhotep (about 2650–2600 BCE) served Pharaoh Djoser as chancellor, high priest of Ra and architect. He designed the Step Pyramid at Saqqara, the first monumental stone building, stacking six mastabas. Later Egyptians credited him with medicine and wisdom, and he was deified; the Greeks identified him with Asclepius.

Rulers & conquerors

Akhenaten

Amenhotep IV (r. about 1353–1336 BCE) renamed himself Akhenaten, promoting the sun disc Aten as the supreme or only god. He closed temples of Amun, built a new capital at Amarna, and encouraged naturalistic art showing his family. His wife was Nefertiti. After his death, his son Tutankhamun restored the old gods and Akhenaten was condemned as a heretic.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Zoroaster

Zoroaster (Zarathustra), a priest-prophet of ancient Iran, probably lived between 1500 and 1000 BCE. He taught that Ahura Mazda, the wise lord, is the supreme god, opposed by the destructive spirit Angra Mainyu, and that humans must choose good thoughts, words and deeds. His hymns, the Gathas, are in the Avesta. His ideas of heaven, hell, judgement and a saviour may have influenced Judaism, Christianity and Islam. Parsis in India follow his faith.

Rulers & conquerors

Bimbisara

Bimbisara (r. about 558–491 BCE) of the Haryanka dynasty made Magadha the strongest state in north India. He annexed Anga, married princesses of Kosala, Vaishali and Madra, and built Rajagriha as capital. He was a patron of the Buddha and is also honoured by Jains. His son Ajatashatru imprisoned him and he died in captivity.

Rulers & conquerors

Pericles

Pericles (about 495–429 BCE), an aristocrat and orator, led Athens during its golden age. He strengthened democracy by paying jurors, used the Delian League's treasury to build the Parthenon and Acropolis, and patronised artists like Phidias. He led Athens into the Peloponnesian War and died in the Plague of Athens.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Herodotus

Herodotus (about 484–425 BCE) was born in Halicarnassus (Bodrum, Turkey), then under Persian rule, and travelled to Egypt, Babylon, Phoenicia, Scythia and across Greece. His Histories, written to explain 'why the Greeks and Persians fought one another', describe the rise of Persia under Cyrus, Darius and Xerxes and the Persian Wars, including Marathon, Thermopylae and Salamis. He collected oral testimony, compared sources and sometimes reported tales he doubted, such as gold-digging ants in India. Cicero called him the 'Father of History', while critics called him the 'Father of Lies'; modern archaeology has confirmed many of his descriptions.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Hippocrates

Hippocrates of Kos (about 460–370 BCE) is called the father of Western medicine. He and his school argued diseases have natural causes rather than divine punishment, and stressed careful observation, diet and the 'four humours'. The Hippocratic Corpus contains about 60 works, and the Hippocratic Oath, with principles like 'do no harm', still inspires medical ethics.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Sun Tzu

Sun Tzu, traditionally a general of the state of Wu in the 6th century BCE, is credited with The Art of War, a 13-chapter treatise. It teaches that the best victory is won without fighting, through deception, intelligence and knowing yourself and the enemy. It influenced Mao, Japanese strategists and modern business.

Rulers & conquerors

Hannibal

Hannibal Barca (247–183 BCE) of Carthage marched an army with elephants across the Pyrenees and Alps in 218 BCE. He destroyed Roman armies at the Trebia, Lake Trasimene and Cannae (216 BCE), where about 50,000 Romans died. He was recalled to defend Carthage and lost to Scipio at Zama (202 BCE). He later took poison to avoid capture.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Euclid

Euclid of Alexandria (about 300 BCE), working in the Ptolemaic capital's Museum, wrote the Elements, 13 books building geometry and number theory from definitions and five postulates. It proved that there are infinitely many primes and constructed the five Platonic solids. It was the main geometry textbook for over 2,000 years, translated into Arabic and Latin.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Sima Qian

Sima Qian (about 145–86 BCE) succeeded his father as Grand Historian at the Han court of Emperor Wu. When he defended General Li Ling, who had surrendered to the Xiongnu, the emperor sentenced him to castration; he accepted this humiliation rather than suicide in order to finish his father's project. His Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), about 130 chapters, covers 2,000 years from the legendary Yellow Emperor to his own time, with annals, tables, treatises and biographies of rulers, rebels, merchants and assassins. It became the model for all later Chinese dynastic histories.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Jesus of Nazareth

Jesus of Nazareth (about 4 BCE – 30/33 CE), a Jewish teacher from Galilee, preached the Kingdom of God, love for enemies and care for the poor, and was known as a healer. He was crucified in Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate. His followers believed he rose from the dead and was the Messiah, founding Christianity, now the world's largest religion.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Prophet Muhammad

Muhammad (about 570–632), born in Mecca, an orphan raised by his uncle, became a trader. From about 610 he received revelations that became the Quran. Persecuted, he moved to Medina in 622 (the Hijra), led the community through battles with Mecca, and took Mecca in 630. By his death Arabia was united under Islam.

Rulers & conquerors

Empress Wu Zetian

Wu Zetian (624–705) was a concubine of Emperor Taizong who became Emperor Gaozong's empress, ruling behind him and then for her sons. In 690 she declared her own Zhou dynasty, China's only female emperor. She expanded the examination system, promoted Buddhism and used secret police. She was deposed in 705.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Xuanzang

Xuanzang (602–664), a Chinese Buddhist monk, left Chang'an in 629 without permission to seek authentic texts. He crossed the Gobi and Central Asia, studied for years at Nalanda, debated at the court of Harsha, and returned in 645 with 657 texts, which he spent his life translating. His Great Tang Records on the Western Regions guided archaeologists to Indian sites, and he inspired the novel Journey to the West.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Ramanuja

Ramanuja (traditionally 1017–1137) was a Tamil Vaishnava philosopher who taught Vishishtadvaita ('qualified non-dualism'): the soul and world are real, distinct parts of Brahman (Vishnu), and liberation comes through devotion (bhakti) and surrender. He reorganised worship at Srirangam and, according to tradition, shared the sacred mantra publicly from a temple tower so all castes could be saved. He is said to have fled Chola persecution to the Hoysala court, where he converted King Vishnuvardhana.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Basava

Basava (Basavanna, 1105–1167), a minister of the Kalachuri king Bijjala at Kalyana in Karnataka, founded the Lingayat (Veerashaiva) movement. He rejected caste, temple ritual and gender discrimination, teaching devotion to Shiva symbolised by a personal linga, and that 'work is worship' (kayaka). His Anubhava Mantapa gathered saints of all castes, including women like Akka Mahadevi, who composed vachanas, free-verse poems in Kannada. A marriage between a Brahmin girl and a Dalit boy sparked violence that ended the community at Kalyana.

Rulers & conquerors

Rani Rudramadevi

Rudramadevi (ruled about 1262–1289) was one of the few women to rule an Indian kingdom in her own right. Her father, the Kakatiya king Ganapati, named her heir and she was raised as a prince. She defeated rebellious nobles and invasions by the Yadavas of Devagiri and the Eastern Gangas, completed Warangal fort, and built tanks for irrigation. Marco Polo praised her rule. Her grandson Prataparudra succeeded her.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Amir Khusrau

Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), a poet, musician and Sufi disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya in Delhi, served seven Delhi sultans. He wrote in Persian and Hindavi, composing ghazals, riddles and histories of Alauddin Khalji's campaigns. He is credited with developing qawwali and the tarana, and legends credit him with inventing the sitar and tabla, though historians doubt this. He called himself the 'Parrot of India'.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Ibn Khaldun

Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), born in Tunis, was a scholar, judge and diplomat in North Africa, Spain and Egypt. His Muqaddimah (1377) analysed why dynasties rise and fall: tribal groups with strong solidarity (asabiyyah) conquer settled societies, then grow soft and lose power within about four generations. He is seen as a founder of sociology and economics. In 1401 he met Timur outside Damascus.

Rulers & conquerors

Zheng He

Zheng He (1371–1433), born to a Muslim family in Yunnan, was captured as a boy by Ming armies, castrated and became a eunuch servant of Prince Zhu Di (later the Yongle Emperor). He commanded seven 'treasure fleet' voyages (1405–1433) with up to 300 ships and 27,000 men, visiting Java, Sri Lanka, Calicut, Hormuz, Mecca and East Africa, and bringing back envoys and a giraffe. After his death, the Ming abandoned the voyages.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Joan of Arc

Joan of Arc (about 1412–1431), a peasant girl from Domrémy, said saints' voices told her to help Charles VII drive the English out of France during the Hundred Years' War. At 17 she led troops to lift the siege of Orléans (1429) and saw Charles crowned at Reims. Captured by Burgundians and handed to the English, she was tried for heresy and burned at Rouen at 19. She was declared innocent in 1456 and made a saint in 1920.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Rana Pratap

Maharana Pratap Singh (1540–1597) of Mewar refused to accept Mughal overlordship, unlike many Rajput rulers. At Haldighati in 1576 he fought Akbar's army led by Man Singh; his horse Chetak carried him to safety before dying. Living in the Aravalli hills with Bhil allies and supported by the merchant Bhamashah's wealth, he waged guerrilla war and regained much of Mewar except Chittor. He remains a symbol of Rajput pride.

Rulers & conquerors

Chand Bibi

Chand Bibi (1550–1599), a princess of Ahmadnagar married into Bijapur's royal family, served as regent of Bijapur and later of Ahmadnagar. In 1595 she defended Ahmadnagar fort against Akbar's son Murad, rallying troops and repairing a breached wall overnight, forcing a treaty. She was killed in 1599, reportedly by her own soldiers, before the Mughals took the fort.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Rani Abbakka Chowta

Abbakka Chowta, the 16th-century Tuluva queen of Ullal near Mangalore, resisted Portuguese attempts to control the coast's trade and demand tribute. A Jain who ruled a mixed Hindu–Muslim–Jain population, she allied with the Zamorin of Calicut and Bijapur. She fought the Portuguese for decades, reportedly using fire arrows at night, before being captured. She is celebrated as one of India's first freedom fighters.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Lachit Borphukan

Lachit Borphukan (1622–1672) was commander of the Ahom army. In 1671, though seriously ill, he led the Ahoms to defeat a much larger Mughal fleet under Raja Ram Singh at the Battle of Saraighat on the Brahmaputra near Guwahati, using river warfare and fortifications. Legends say he beheaded his own uncle for failing to complete a defensive wall overnight. Lachit Divas is celebrated on 24 November.

Rulers & conquerors

Ahilyabai Holkar

Ahilyabai Holkar (1725–1795) was widowed at 29 and lost her father-in-law Malhar Rao and son, then took over as ruler of the Holkar state of Malwa from Maheshwar. She ruled for 28 years with justice and prosperity, defended her lands and built or restored hundreds of temples, ghats and rest houses across India, including the Kashi Vishwanath temple in Varanasi (1780) and Somnath. Indore's airport and university are named after her.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Toussaint Louverture

François-Dominique Toussaint Louverture (about 1743–1803), born enslaved in Saint-Domingue, gained his freedom and became the leader of the 1791 slave revolt. A skilled general, he defeated Spanish and British forces, abolished slavery and governed the colony. Napoleon sent an army to restore slavery; Toussaint was captured by trickery and died in a French prison in 1803. Haiti won independence months later.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Simón Bolívar

Simón Bolívar (1783–1830), a wealthy Venezuelan creole, swore to free South America from Spanish rule. After defeats and exile, he crossed the Andes in 1819 to win at Boyacá, then won at Carabobo (1821) and with Sucre at Ayacucho (1824). He liberated Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru and Bolivia (named after him) and led Gran Colombia, but his dream of a united South America collapsed amid regional rivalry before his death.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Velu Nachiyar

Velu Nachiyar (1730–1796), princess of Ramanathapuram, was trained in martial arts and languages. After the East India Company and the Nawab of Arcot killed her husband, the king of Sivaganga, in 1772, she escaped, allied with Hyder Ali, and in 1780 recaptured Sivaganga. Legend says her commander Kuyili set herself on fire to destroy a British ammunition store. She is called Veeramangai ('brave woman').

Writers & poets

William Shakespeare

Born in Stratford-upon-Avon to a glove-maker, Shakespeare moved to London around 1590 and became an actor, playwright and shareholder in the Lord Chamberlain's Men, who built the Globe Theatre in 1599. He wrote about 39 plays — tragedies (Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, Macbeth), comedies (A Midsummer Night's Dream, Twelfth Night) and histories (Henry V, Richard III) — and 154 sonnets. He coined or popularised hundreds of English words and phrases. His plays, performed under Elizabeth I and James I, are translated into every major language and staged worldwide, including countless Indian adaptations such as Vishal Bhardwaj's Maqbool, Omkara and Haider.

Writers & poets

Munshi Premchand

Born Dhanpat Rai Srivastava in Lamhi near Varanasi, Premchand worked as a schoolteacher and inspector before resigning in 1921 in response to Gandhi's Non-Cooperation call. He wrote first in Urdu (his 1907 story collection Soz-e-Watan was banned and burned by the British) and then Hindi, producing about 300 stories and over a dozen novels. His works — Godaan, Gaban, Nirmala, and stories like Idgah, Kafan and Poos Ki Raat — depicted peasants, caste oppression, poverty, dowry and colonial exploitation with stark realism. He presided over the first Progressive Writers' Association meeting in 1936 and is called 'Upanyas Samrat' (emperor of novels).

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Marilyn Monroe

Born Norma Jeane Mortenson in Los Angeles, Monroe spent much of her childhood in foster homes and an orphanage. She became Hollywood's biggest star of the 1950s in films like Gentlemen Prefer Blondes, The Seven Year Itch and Some Like It Hot (Golden Globe, 1960). She founded her own production company in 1955 — rare for an actress — to fight studio control, and studied at the Actors Studio. Her struggles with fame, depression and addiction, her marriages to Joe DiMaggio and Arthur Miller, and her death from a barbiturate overdose at 36 made her an enduring icon of glamour and of the costs of celebrity.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Charlie Chaplin

Born into poverty in London, Chaplin performed on stage from childhood and went to the US in 1910. His 'Little Tramp' character, with bowler hat, cane and moustache, made him the world's most famous film star in silent films like The Kid (1921), The Gold Rush (1925), City Lights (1931) and Modern Times (1936), which satirised industrial life. In The Great Dictator (1940) he mocked Hitler. Accused of communist sympathies during the McCarthy era, he was barred from re-entering the US in 1952 and settled in Switzerland. He met Gandhi in London in 1931 and discussed machines and labour.

Writers & poets

Valmiki

Valmiki is honoured as the adi kavi ('first poet') of Sanskrit and the author of the Ramayana, the 24,000-verse epic of Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman. Legend says he was once a robber named Ratnakara who became a sage after meditating so long that an anthill (valmika) grew over him. The epic, composed over centuries, spread across Asia in countless versions, from Tamil Kamban and Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas to Thailand's Ramakien and Indonesia's Kakawin Ramayana.

Writers & poets

Vyasa

Vyasa (Krishna Dvaipayana) is traditionally the compiler of the Vedas and the author of the Mahabharata, the world's longest epic (about 100,000 couplets), which includes the Bhagavad Gita. Legend says Ganesha wrote it down as Vyasa dictated. The epic tells of the war at Kurukshetra between the Pandavas and Kauravas and explores dharma, duty and fate.

Writers & poets

Homer

Homer is the legendary blind poet credited with the Iliad, about the wrath of Achilles in the Trojan War, and the Odyssey, about Odysseus's ten-year journey home. The epics grew out of oral tradition and were written down after the Greek alphabet was adopted. They shaped Greek education, religion and identity, and remain foundational works of Western literature.

Writers & poets

Tulsidas

Tulsidas (1532–1623) was born in Rajapur in Uttar Pradesh and, according to legend, was abandoned as an inauspicious child and raised by a servant woman. After his wife rebuked his attachment to her, he became a devotee of Rama and spent much of his life in Varanasi and Ayodhya. He wrote the Ramcharitmanas (1574–1577) in Awadhi, retelling Valmiki's Ramayana in a devotional form accessible to ordinary people; it became the most widely read religious text in north India. He also wrote the Hanuman Chalisa and the Vinaya Patrika, and his poetry is the basis of the Ramlila performances held every Dussehra.

Writers & poets

Surdas

Surdas (about 1478–1583) was a poet-saint of the Bhakti movement who, according to tradition, was blind, perhaps from birth. A disciple of Vallabhacharya, he sang in the temple of Shrinathji at Govardhan. His Sur Sagar, written in Braj Bhasha, contains thousands of songs, especially about Krishna's childhood in Vrindavan — stealing butter, playing with the gopis — and the longing of Radha and the gopis after he left. His tender lyrical style made Braj Bhasha the main literary language of north India for centuries; legend says Akbar visited him.

Writers & poets

Mirza Ghalib

Mirza Asadullah Baig Khan, 'Ghalib' (1797–1869), was born in Agra to a family of Turkish soldiers and lived most of his life in Delhi. He wrote ghazals in Urdu and Persian full of wit, scepticism, love and philosophical depth, and served as poet-tutor to Bahadur Shah Zafar, the last Mughal emperor. He witnessed the 1857 uprising and the British sack of Delhi; his letters and Persian diary Dastanbuy describe the devastation. Often in debt and denied his pension, he remains the most quoted Urdu poet, with verses such as 'Hazaron khwahishen aisi ki har khwahish pe dam nikle'.

Writers & poets

Allama Iqbal

Muhammad Iqbal (1877–1938), born in Sialkot, studied philosophy at Cambridge and earned a doctorate in Munich. He wrote poetry in Urdu and Persian, including 'Saare Jahan Se Achha' (1904), still sung in India, and the Persian Asrar-i-Khudi on self-development (khudi). Later he became concerned with the revival of the Muslim world; in his 1930 presidential address to the Muslim League at Allahabad he proposed a Muslim state in north-west India, an idea that influenced the Pakistan movement. He is Pakistan's national poet, and his poetry is also loved in India and Iran.

Writers & poets

Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay

Bankim Chandra, one of the first graduates of Calcutta University and a colonial official, wrote the first major Bengali novels. His Anandamath (1882), set during the Sannyasi rebellion, contained the song 'Vande Mataram', which became a rallying cry of the freedom movement and India's national song.

Writers & poets

Subramania Bharati

Subramania Bharati (1882–1921), born in Ettayapuram, Tamil Nadu, mastered Tamil, Sanskrit, Hindi and English. As a journalist with newspapers like Swadesamitran and India, he wrote fiery patriotic poems such as 'Achamillai' ('No fear') that inspired the freedom movement. Facing arrest, he lived in French-ruled Pondicherry from 1908 to 1918, where he met Aurobindo. He championed women's education, opposed caste and child marriage, and modernised Tamil poetry with simple language. He died at 38 after being injured by a temple elephant; his works were nationalised by the Tamil Nadu government in 1949.

Writers & poets

Rumi

Jalal al-Din Rumi, born in Balkh, fled the Mongols and settled in Konya. His friendship with the wandering mystic Shams of Tabriz transformed him into an ecstatic poet. His Masnavi, 25,000 Persian couplets, is called 'the Quran in Persian', and his followers founded the Mevlevi 'whirling dervishes'.

Writers & poets

Ferdowsi

Abu'l-Qasim Ferdowsi (940–1020) was a landowner from Tus in Khorasan who spent about 30 years writing the Shahnameh ('Book of Kings'), about 50,000 couplets, completed in 1010. It retells Iran's history from the creation of the world through mythical kings and heroes like Rostam and Sohrab to the fall of the Sasanian Empire to the Arabs. Written in Persian with very few Arabic words, it helped preserve the Persian language and identity after the Islamic conquest. Legend says Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni paid him in silver rather than the promised gold. The Shahnameh influenced Persian, Turkish and Mughal culture, and its illustrated manuscripts are among the masterpieces of Persian painting.

Writers & poets

Dante Alighieri

Dante Alighieri (1265–1321) was a Florentine poet and politician who was exiled in 1302 after his political faction lost power, and never returned. In exile he wrote the Divine Comedy, an epic in which the poet Virgil guides Dante through Hell (Inferno) and Purgatory, and his beloved Beatrice guides him through Paradise. Written in Tuscan Italian rather than Latin, it helped create the Italian literary language. Dante placed popes and political enemies in Hell, and the work's vision of the afterlife shaped European imagination. He died in Ravenna, where he is buried.

Writers & poets

Miguel de Cervantes

Miguel de Cervantes (1547–1616) fought at the naval Battle of Lepanto (1571) against the Ottomans, where he lost the use of his left hand. Pirates captured him in 1575, and he spent five years as a captive in Algiers, attempting four escapes before being ransomed. Back in Spain he worked as a tax collector and was jailed for accounting irregularities. He wrote Don Quixote (1605, 1615), about a man who reads too many chivalric romances and sets out as a knight with his squire Sancho Panza; it is often called the first modern novel. He died within a day of Shakespeare's death in 1616.

Writers & poets

Leo Tolstoy

Count Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910) was born on his family estate, Yasnaya Polyana, served in the Crimean War and wrote the Sevastopol Sketches. His novels War and Peace (1869), set during Napoleon's invasion of Russia, and Anna Karenina (1877) are among the greatest ever written. In middle age he had a spiritual crisis and embraced a radical Christian ethic of non-violence, simplicity and rejection of property and the state, and was excommunicated by the Orthodox Church. His 'Letter to a Hindu' (1908) and correspondence with Gandhi influenced satyagraha; Gandhi named his commune in South Africa Tolstoy Farm. He died at a railway station after leaving home aged 82.

Writers & poets

Fyodor Dostoevsky

Fyodor Dostoevsky (1821–1881) joined a radical intellectual circle and was arrested in 1849; he faced a mock execution before being sentenced to four years of hard labour in Siberia, an experience recounted in Notes from the House of the Dead. Struggling with epilepsy, gambling debts and poverty, he wrote Crime and Punishment (1866), The Idiot, Demons and The Brothers Karamazov (1880), exploring guilt, faith, free will and the dangers of radical ideologies. His psychological depth influenced existentialism and modern fiction.

Writers & poets

Victor Hugo

Victor Hugo (1802–1885) was a leading figure of French Romanticism, writing poetry, plays and novels. The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831) helped save the cathedral by reviving interest in Gothic architecture. A supporter of the 1848 Republic, he denounced Louis-Napoléon's coup in 1851 and spent 19 years in exile on Jersey and Guernsey, where he finished Les Misérables (1862), a story of the ex-convict Jean Valjean, poverty and the June Rebellion of 1832. He campaigned against the death penalty and for universal education. Two million people attended his funeral in Paris.

Writers & poets

Charles Dickens

Charles Dickens (1812–1870) was sent at 12 to work in a boot-blacking factory when his father was imprisoned for debt, an experience that shaped his concern for the poor. He became a journalist and then the most popular novelist of Victorian Britain, publishing in monthly instalments: The Pickwick Papers, Oliver Twist (workhouses and child crime), A Christmas Carol (1843), David Copperfield, Bleak House, Hard Times (industrial towns), A Tale of Two Cities (the French Revolution) and Great Expectations. His vivid characters such as Scrooge and Fagin, and his criticism of social injustice, influenced reforms. He gave reading tours in Britain and America.

Writers & poets

Jane Austen

Jane Austen (1775–1817), a clergyman's daughter in Hampshire, wrote six novels — Sense and Sensibility (1811), Pride and Prejudice (1813), Mansfield Park, Emma, Northanger Abbey and Persuasion — published anonymously ('By a Lady'). With irony and wit she portrayed the gentry, marriage, money and women's limited options in Georgian England, set against the backdrop of the Napoleonic Wars. She never married and died at 41. Her novels pioneered free indirect speech and remain hugely popular through films and adaptations.

Writers & poets

Mark Twain

Samuel Clemens (1835–1910) grew up in Hannibal, Missouri, on the Mississippi, where he became a riverboat pilot; his pen name means 'two fathoms deep'. As a journalist and humorist he wrote The Adventures of Tom Sawyer (1876) and Adventures of Huckleberry Finn (1884), in which the boy Huck helps Jim, an escaped slave, a novel Hemingway said all modern American literature comes from. He criticised racism, imperialism and hypocrisy. In 1896 he toured India and wrote in Following the Equator that India was 'the cradle of the human race'.

Writers & poets

Lu Xun

Lu Xun (pen name of Zhou Shuren, 1881–1936) studied medicine in Japan but turned to literature after seeing a slide of Chinese spectators watching passively as a countryman was executed. His 'A Madman's Diary' (1918), the first major story in vernacular Chinese, attacked Confucian tradition as 'cannibalism'. 'The True Story of Ah Q' (1921) satirised Chinese apathy after the 1911 revolution. He is considered the father of modern Chinese literature and was later championed by Mao.

Writers & poets

Murasaki Shikibu

Murasaki Shikibu (about 973–1014) was a lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi at the Heian court in Kyoto. Educated in Chinese classics by eavesdropping on her brother's lessons, she wrote The Tale of Genji, about the loves and life of the 'Shining Prince' Genji, in 54 chapters using Japanese kana script. It portrays court life, poetry and the Buddhist sense of impermanence (mono no aware). Often called the world's first novel, it has been illustrated in scrolls since the 12th century. She also kept a diary of court life.

Writers & poets

Chinua Achebe

Chinua Achebe (1930–2013), an Igbo writer from Nigeria, wrote Things Fall Apart (1958), the story of Okonkwo, a proud leader whose world collapses under British colonialism and Christian missionaries. Written partly in response to European portrayals of Africa, such as Conrad's Heart of Darkness, which he criticised as racist, it has sold over 20 million copies in 50 languages. He wrote Arrow of God and No Longer at Ease, supported Biafra in the Nigerian civil war, and taught in the US after a car accident left him paralysed.

Writers & poets

Gabriel García Márquez

Gabriel García Márquez (1927–2014), known as 'Gabo', was a Colombian journalist and novelist raised by his grandparents in Aracataca, whose stories inspired his fiction. One Hundred Years of Solitude (1967), the saga of the Buendía family in the fictional town of Macondo, made 'magical realism' famous and sold tens of millions of copies. He also wrote Love in the Time of Cholera and Chronicle of a Death Foretold, and received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1982. His work reflects Latin America's history of colonialism, civil wars and dictatorship.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Michelangelo

Michelangelo Buonarroti carved the Pietà (1499) and the 5-metre David (1504) from a block other sculptors had abandoned. For Pope Julius II he painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling (1508–12) lying on scaffolding, and later The Last Judgement. As architect he designed the dome of St Peter's Basilica. He was the first artist whose biography was written in his lifetime.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Vincent van Gogh

Vincent van Gogh (1853–1890), a Dutch pastor's son, worked as an art dealer and lay preacher among coal miners before becoming a painter at 27. Influenced by Impressionism and Japanese prints in Paris, he moved to Arles in southern France in 1888 and painted brilliant, emotional works with thick brushstrokes: Sunflowers, The Bedroom, Café Terrace at Night. After a breakdown in which he cut off part of his ear, he entered an asylum at Saint-Rémy, where he painted The Starry Night (1889). Supported financially by his brother Theo, he sold almost nothing in his lifetime and died of a gunshot wound at 37.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Pablo Picasso

Pablo Picasso (1881–1973), born in Málaga, Spain, was a child prodigy who spent most of his life in France. His Blue Period and Rose Period gave way to Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907), inspired partly by African masks, and with Georges Braque he founded Cubism, showing objects from many viewpoints at once. In 1937 he painted Guernica in response to the bombing of the Basque town by Nazi and Italian planes during the Spanish Civil War; it became the century's most famous anti-war image. He produced about 50,000 works in painting, sculpture, ceramics and prints.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791) was born in Salzburg and toured Europe's courts as a child prodigy with his father Leopold and sister Nannerl, composing from age five. Moving to Vienna, he wrote over 600 works, including 41 symphonies, 27 piano concertos and operas such as The Marriage of Figaro (1786), Don Giovanni (1787) and The Magic Flute (1791). Despite success he was often in debt. He died at 35 while composing his Requiem, leaving it unfinished; rumours that rival Salieri poisoned him are unfounded.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Ludwig van Beethoven

Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827) was born in Bonn and moved to Vienna, where he studied briefly with Haydn. From his late twenties he began to lose his hearing, writing in the 1802 Heiligenstadt Testament of his despair, yet he composed his greatest works when almost completely deaf. His nine symphonies, including the Third ('Eroica'), originally dedicated to Napoleon until he crowned himself emperor, the Fifth, and the Ninth with its 'Ode to Joy' (now the European anthem), bridged Classical and Romantic music. His piano sonatas and late string quartets remain landmarks.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Tansen

Tansen (about 1500–1586), born Ramtanu near Gwalior, trained with Swami Haridas of Vrindavan and in the dhrupad tradition of Raja Man Singh Tomar's Gwalior court. He served the Rewa court before Akbar summoned him in 1562; he became one of the nine jewels (navaratnas) of Akbar's court. He composed ragas such as Miyan ki Malhar, Miyan ki Todi and Darbari Kanada, and legends say his singing of raga Deepak lit lamps and Megh Malhar brought rain. His tomb in Gwalior hosts the Tansen Samaroh music festival every December.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Raja Ravi Varma

Raja Ravi Varma (1848–1906), from the royal family of Kilimanoor in Travancore, learned oil painting from a visiting Dutch-trained artist. He painted portraits of royalty and scenes from the Mahabharata, Ramayana and Kalidasa's works, such as Shakuntala and Hamsa Damayanti, combining European realism with Indian subjects and sarees. In 1894 he set up a lithographic press near Mumbai, producing cheap colour prints of gods and goddesses that reached ordinary homes and shaped how Indians imagine their deities. Some critics accused him of Westernising Indian art.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Dadasaheb Phalke

Dhundiraj Govind Phalke (1870–1944) was a printer, photographer and magician in Maharashtra. Inspired by watching The Life of Christ in a Bombay cinema in 1911, he pawned his wife's jewellery to learn filmmaking in London. He made India's first full-length feature film, Raja Harishchandra (1913), a mythological drama in which men played female roles because women were unwilling to act. He made about 95 films and 26 shorts, including Lanka Dahan and Kaliya Mardan, but was ruined by the coming of sound. India's highest cinema honour, the Dadasaheb Phalke Award, is named after him.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Satyajit Ray

Satyajit Ray (1921–1992), born in Kolkata to a family of writers, studied at Tagore's Santiniketan and worked as a graphic designer. Inspired by Italian neorealism (Bicycle Thieves), he made Pather Panchali (1955), about a poor Bengali boy, Apu, with non-professional actors and a tiny budget; it won a prize at Cannes. Ray went on to make Aparajito and Apur Sansar (the Apu Trilogy), Charulata, Jalsaghar and Shatranj Ke Khilari. He wrote the Feluda detective stories and Professor Shonku science fiction, and received an honorary Academy Award and the Bharat Ratna in 1992.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Lata Mangeshkar

Lata Mangeshkar (1929–2022), daughter of the musician Deenanath Mangeshkar, began singing to support her family after her father's death when she was 13. She became the leading playback singer of Indian cinema from the late 1940s, recording thousands of songs in over 36 languages for films from Mahal (1949) to the 2000s. Her rendition of 'Ae Mere Watan Ke Logon' (1963), honouring soldiers who died in the war with China, reportedly moved Nehru to tears. She was awarded the Bharat Ratna in 2001 and called the 'Nightingale of India'.

Artists, musicians & entertainers

Elvis Presley

Elvis Presley (1935–1977) grew up poor in Tupelo, Mississippi and Memphis, Tennessee, absorbing gospel, country and Black rhythm and blues. His recordings at Sun Records from 1954 and hits like 'Heartbreak Hotel', 'Hound Dog' and 'Jailhouse Rock' made him the 'King of Rock and Roll'; his hip-shaking performances scandalised conservatives. His success also raised debates about white artists profiting from Black music. He served in the US Army in Germany, starred in 31 films, and died at 42.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Immanuel Kant

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) spent his entire life in Königsberg, Prussia, famous for his punctual daily walks. In the Critique of Pure Reason (1781) he argued that the mind actively structures experience through categories like space, time and causality, so we can know things only as they appear to us, not as they are in themselves. In ethics he proposed the categorical imperative: act only according to rules you could will to be universal laws, and treat people as ends, never merely as means. His essay 'What is Enlightenment?' (1784) urged 'Sapere aude — dare to know', and he proposed a federation of states for perpetual peace.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

René Descartes

René Descartes (1596–1650), a French philosopher and mathematician, served as a soldier during the Thirty Years' War and lived mostly in the Netherlands. In Discourse on the Method (1637) and Meditations, he doubted everything he could and found certainty in 'I think, therefore I am'. He argued mind and body are distinct substances (Cartesian dualism). He invented analytic geometry, the Cartesian coordinate system linking algebra and geometry. He died in Stockholm after being invited to tutor Queen Christina.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Karl Marx

Karl Marx (1818–1883), born in Trier, studied philosophy and became a radical journalist; expelled from Germany, France and Belgium, he settled in London in 1849, supported by his friend Friedrich Engels. With Engels he wrote The Communist Manifesto (1848), declaring 'Workers of the world, unite!'. In Das Kapital (1867) he argued that capitalism exploits workers by extracting surplus value and would be replaced by socialism. He also wrote on British rule in India, criticising its brutality while seeing it as destroying old structures. His ideas inspired the Russian and Chinese revolutions and labour movements worldwide.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Friedrich Nietzsche

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) became a professor of classics at Basel at 24 but retired due to illness. In works like Thus Spoke Zarathustra and Beyond Good and Evil he declared 'God is dead', criticised Christian morality as a 'slave morality', and imagined the Übermensch who creates his own values, and the 'eternal recurrence'. He suffered a mental collapse in 1889. His sister Elisabeth, a Nazi sympathiser, edited his writings to suit antisemitic nationalism, although Nietzsche had despised antisemitism.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Jean-Jacques Rousseau

Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778), born in Geneva, was a self-taught philosopher, composer and novelist. His Discourse on Inequality argued that private property and civilisation corrupted naturally good humans. The Social Contract (1762) opened 'Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains' and argued legitimate government rests on the people's 'general will'. Émile proposed natural education. His ideas inspired the French Revolution and Romanticism.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Voltaire

François-Marie Arouet, 'Voltaire' (1694–1778), was a French writer imprisoned in the Bastille for satire and exiled to England, where he admired its freedoms. He championed religious tolerance and free speech, campaigning for victims of injustice like Jean Calas. His satirical novel Candide (1759) mocked naive optimism. He corresponded with Frederick the Great and Catherine the Great, and wrote Essay on the Customs and Spirit of Nations, which praised India and China.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

John Locke

John Locke (1632–1704), an English physician and philosopher, lived in exile in the Netherlands for political reasons and returned after the Glorious Revolution of 1688. In Two Treatises of Government he argued that people have natural rights to life, liberty and property, that governments exist by the consent of the governed, and that people may overthrow a government that violates their rights. In An Essay Concerning Human Understanding he argued the mind is a 'blank slate' shaped by experience. His ideas influenced the US Declaration of Independence. He also helped draft the constitution of the Carolina colony and invested in the slave trade, a contradiction critics note.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Niccolò Machiavelli

Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527) served as a diplomat and official of the Florentine Republic until the Medici returned in 1512; he was imprisoned and tortured, then exiled to his farm. There he wrote The Prince (1513), advising rulers to be practical, use deception when necessary and, if they cannot be both, to be feared rather than loved. His Discourses on Livy praised republican government. 'Machiavellian' became a byword for cunning, but scholars see him as a founder of realist political science, often compared with India's Chanakya.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Mencius

Mencius (Mengzi, about 372–289 BCE), the most important Confucian after Confucius, was raised by a famously devoted mother who moved house three times to give him good surroundings. He travelled among the Warring States advising rulers. He taught that human nature is good, with innate sprouts of compassion, shame, respect and right and wrong, which must be cultivated. He argued that the people are most important and that a tyrant who oppresses them loses the Mandate of Heaven and may be overthrown. The Mencius became one of the Four Books of the Confucian canon.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Nagarjuna

Nagarjuna (about 150–250 CE) was an Indian Buddhist philosopher, traditionally associated with the Satavahana kings and the region of Nagarjunakonda in Andhra Pradesh. In his Mulamadhyamakakarika he founded the Madhyamaka ('Middle Way') school, arguing that all things are empty (shunya) of independent existence because they arise dependently. He used logic to show that fixed views lead to contradictions. He is revered as a 'second Buddha' in Tibetan and East Asian Buddhism.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Louis Pasteur

Louis Pasteur (1822–1895), a French chemist, showed that fermentation and spoilage are caused by microorganisms, disproving spontaneous generation with his swan-neck flask experiment. He developed pasteurisation to keep wine and milk safe, saved the silk industry from silkworm disease, and helped establish the germ theory of disease. He developed vaccines for chicken cholera, anthrax and, in 1885, rabies, first used on a boy bitten by a rabid dog. The Pasteur Institute he founded continues research; the Pasteur Institute of India in Coonoor was founded in 1907.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Thomas Edison

Thomas Edison (1847–1931) had little formal schooling and worked as a telegraph operator before becoming a full-time inventor. At his Menlo Park laboratory, the first industrial research lab, he invented the phonograph (1877), developed a long-lasting carbon-filament light bulb (1879), and built the first commercial power station at Pearl Street, New York (1882). He also worked on motion picture cameras. He held 1,093 US patents. His 'war of the currents' with Tesla and Westinghouse, promoting direct current, was lost to alternating current.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Nikola Tesla

Nikola Tesla (1856–1943), a Serbian born in Croatia, emigrated to the US in 1884 and briefly worked for Edison. He invented the induction motor and the polyphase alternating-current system, which George Westinghouse used to power the 1893 Chicago World's Fair and Niagara Falls power station, making AC the global standard. He experimented with radio, wireless power and X-rays. His Wardenclyffe tower for wireless transmission failed for lack of funds, and he died poor in a New York hotel. The SI unit of magnetic field is named after him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Michael Faraday

Michael Faraday (1791–1867), the son of a blacksmith, had little schooling and learned science while apprenticed to a bookbinder. He became Humphry Davy's assistant at the Royal Institution and discovered electromagnetic rotation (the principle of the electric motor, 1821) and electromagnetic induction (1831), the basis of electric generators and transformers. He also discovered benzene and laws of electrolysis. His idea of fields of force inspired Maxwell's equations. He started the Royal Institution Christmas Lectures for children.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Alan Turing

Alan Turing (1912–1954), a British mathematician, described in 1936 a theoretical 'universal machine' that could compute anything computable, the foundation of computer science. During World War II at Bletchley Park he designed the Bombe machine that broke German Enigma codes, shortening the war. After the war he designed early computers and proposed the Turing test for machine intelligence (1950), and studied pattern formation in biology. Convicted of homosexuality in 1952 and forced to undergo chemical castration, he died of cyanide poisoning in 1954. Britain pardoned him in 2013, and he appears on the £50 note.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Stephen Hawking

Stephen Hawking (1942–2018) was diagnosed with motor neurone disease at 21 and given two years to live, but lived another 55, eventually communicating through a speech computer. With Roger Penrose he showed the universe began in a singularity, and in 1974 predicted that black holes emit radiation (Hawking radiation) because of quantum effects. He held Newton's chair at Cambridge, and his book A Brief History of Time (1988) sold over 25 million copies.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Homi J. Bhabha

Homi Jehangir Bhabha (1909–1966), from a wealthy Parsi family in Bombay, studied at Cambridge and did important work on cosmic rays and particle physics. Trapped in India by World War II, he founded the Tata Institute of Fundamental Research (1945) with J. R. D. Tata's support and, with Nehru's backing, the Atomic Energy Commission (1948) and Atomic Energy Establishment at Trombay (renamed BARC). He planned India's three-stage nuclear power programme using thorium. He died in an Air India crash on Mont Blanc in 1966.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Vikram Sarabhai

Vikram Sarabhai (1919–1971), from an Ahmedabad industrial family, studied at Cambridge and founded the Physical Research Laboratory (1947). After Sputnik he convinced the government that space technology could serve development. He founded INCOSPAR (1962), launched India's first sounding rocket from Thumba near Thiruvananthapuram in 1963 (parts carried by bicycle and bullock cart), and founded ISRO in 1969. He also co-founded IIM Ahmedabad. He died suddenly at 52; the Vikram lander of Chandrayaan is named after him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Jagadish Chandra Bose

Jagadish Chandra Bose (1858–1937), a Bengali physicist and botanist, demonstrated wireless transmission of microwaves in Calcutta in 1895 and invented improved detectors, contributing to radio science; he refused to patent his inventions. He then studied plants, inventing the crescograph to measure tiny growth and showing plants respond to stimuli electrically. He faced discrimination in pay at Presidency College and worked without salary for three years in protest. He founded the Bose Institute in 1917.

Explorers & travellers

Christopher Columbus

Christopher Columbus (1451–1506), a Genoese sailor, believed he could reach Asia by sailing west, underestimating the Earth's size. After rejections by Portugal, Ferdinand and Isabella of Spain sponsored him; on 12 October 1492 his three ships reached the Bahamas, and he went on to Cuba and Hispaniola. On four voyages he explored the Caribbean and Central and South American coasts, still believing he was near Asia. As governor of Hispaniola he enslaved and brutally treated the Taíno, and was arrested in 1500. His voyages began European colonisation of the Americas, with devastating results for Indigenous peoples.

Explorers & travellers

Vasco da Gama

Vasco da Gama (about 1460–1524) commanded the Portuguese fleet that sailed around the Cape of Good Hope, up the East African coast and, with a local pilot from Malindi, across the Arabian Sea to Calicut in May 1498. The Zamorin of Calicut was unimpressed by his gifts, but the spices he brought back were worth 60 times the cost. On his second voyage (1502) he bombarded Calicut and burned a pilgrim ship with hundreds aboard. He returned as Viceroy of India in 1524 and died in Cochin.

Explorers & travellers

Ferdinand Magellan

Ferdinand Magellan (1480–1521), a Portuguese navigator who had served in India and Malacca, sailed for Spain in 1519 with five ships to reach the Spice Islands by sailing west. He suppressed a mutiny, discovered the Strait of Magellan at South America's tip, and crossed the Pacific, which he named, as his crew starved. In the Philippines he was killed in the Battle of Mactan (1521) by the forces of chief Lapulapu. Under Juan Sebastián Elcano, one ship and 18 men returned in 1522, completing the first circumnavigation.

Rulers & conquerors

Abraham Lincoln

Born in a Kentucky log cabin and largely self-taught, Lincoln became a lawyer and was elected president in 1860 on a platform against slavery's expansion. Southern states seceded, and he led the Union through the Civil War, issuing the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and pushing the 13th Amendment abolishing slavery. His Gettysburg Address defined democracy as 'government of the people, by the people, for the people'. He was assassinated by John Wilkes Booth days after the war ended.

Rulers & conquerors

George Washington

Washington commanded the Continental Army, surviving defeats and the winter at Valley Forge, and won with French help at Yorktown (1781). He presided over the Constitutional Convention and served as first President (1789–97), voluntarily stepping down after two terms. He owned enslaved people, freeing them in his will.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Vladimir Lenin

Vladimir Ulyanov 'Lenin' (1870–1924) became a revolutionary after his elder brother was hanged for plotting to kill Tsar Alexander III. He led the Bolshevik faction, arguing for a disciplined party of professional revolutionaries. After the February Revolution of 1917, Germany let him travel from Switzerland to Russia in a sealed train; in October the Bolsheviks seized power. He made peace with Germany, won the civil war using the Cheka secret police and Red Terror, and introduced the New Economic Policy. He founded the Soviet Union in 1922 and died after strokes in 1924.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Mustafa Kemal Atatürk

Mustafa Kemal (1881–1938), born in Ottoman Salonica, became famous defending Gallipoli against Allied landings in 1915. After the Ottoman defeat he organised the Turkish National Movement from Ankara and won the War of Independence (1919–1922) against Greek and Allied forces. He proclaimed the Republic of Turkey in 1923 and served as its first president, abolishing the sultanate and caliphate, closing religious courts, replacing Arabic script with the Latin alphabet, introducing surnames ('Atatürk' means 'father of the Turks') and giving women the vote (1934). His reforms were imposed in an authoritarian way, including the suppression of Kurdish revolts.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Sun Yat-sen

Sun Yat-sen (1866–1925) was educated in Hawaii and Hong Kong as a doctor. He founded revolutionary societies against the Qing dynasty, staged failed uprisings and raised funds among overseas Chinese. After the Wuchang Uprising of 1911 he was elected provisional president of the Republic of China but soon gave way to general Yuan Shikai. He founded the Kuomintang and allied with the Soviet Union and the Communists. His Three Principles of the People — nationalism, democracy and livelihood — are honoured in both China and Taiwan.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Ho Chi Minh

Ho Chi Minh (1890–1969) left Vietnam in 1911 as a ship's cook and lived in France, the US, Britain, the Soviet Union and China, becoming a founding member of the French Communist Party. In 1941 he returned to lead the Viet Minh against Japanese occupation, and on 2 September 1945 he declared Vietnamese independence, quoting the US Declaration. He fought France until its defeat at Dien Bien Phu (1954), then led North Vietnam against the US-backed South; he died before reunification in 1975, when Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Che Guevara

Ernesto 'Che' Guevara (1928–1967), an Argentine medical student, travelled across Latin America on a motorcycle and was radicalised by poverty and US intervention, including the 1954 coup in Guatemala. In Mexico he joined Fidel Castro, and they launched the guerrilla war that overthrew Batista in 1959. He served as Cuba's industry minister and central bank head, and ordered executions of former regime officials. He visited India in 1959, meeting Nehru. He left Cuba to spread revolution in Congo and Bolivia, where he was captured and executed in 1967; his portrait became a global icon.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Fidel Castro

Fidel Castro (1926–2016), a lawyer, led a failed attack on the Moncada barracks in 1953, then waged a guerrilla war from the Sierra Maestra, taking power on 1 January 1959. He nationalised industries and allied with the Soviet Union, survived the US-backed Bay of Pigs invasion (1961) and many assassination attempts, and brought the world close to nuclear war in the 1962 Missile Crisis. Cuba achieved high literacy and health care but jailed dissidents and saw mass emigration. He ruled until 2006, handing power to his brother Raúl.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Harriet Tubman

Harriet Tubman (about 1822–1913) was born into slavery in Maryland, suffered a severe head injury from an overseer, and escaped to Philadelphia in 1849. She returned about 13 times to lead some 70 enslaved people, including family members, to freedom along the Underground Railroad, never losing a passenger. During the Civil War she served the Union as a scout and spy and guided the Combahee River Raid that freed over 700 people. Later she supported women's suffrage and cared for elderly African Americans.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Frederick Douglass

Frederick Douglass (1818–1895) was born into slavery in Maryland, secretly learned to read, and escaped in 1838 disguised as a sailor. His autobiography, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (1845), and powerful speeches — such as 'What to the Slave is the Fourth of July?' — made him the leading Black abolitionist. He advised Lincoln, recruited Black soldiers for the Union, supported women's rights at the Seneca Falls convention, and later served as US minister to Haiti.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Rosa Parks

Rosa Parks (1913–2005) was a seamstress and secretary of the Montgomery branch of the NAACP in Alabama, where segregation laws forced Black passengers to give up bus seats to white riders. On 1 December 1955 she refused, and was arrested. Her arrest sparked the Montgomery bus boycott, led by the young pastor Martin Luther King Jr., which lasted 381 days until the Supreme Court ruled bus segregation unconstitutional. She faced job loss and threats, moved to Detroit, and was later called 'the mother of the civil rights movement'.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Emmeline Pankhurst

Emmeline Pankhurst (1858–1928) founded the Women's Social and Political Union in Manchester in 1903 with her daughters, adopting the motto 'Deeds, not words'. Suffragettes smashed windows, chained themselves to railings and committed arson; jailed, they went on hunger strike and were force-fed. She suspended the campaign during World War I to support the war effort. Women over 30 won the vote in 1918, and women gained equal voting rights in 1928, weeks after her death.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Maximilien Robespierre

Maximilien Robespierre (1758–1794), a provincial lawyer inspired by Rousseau, became a leader of the radical Jacobins during the French Revolution, known as 'the Incorruptible'. He opposed the death penalty early on but argued for executing Louis XVI. As the leading member of the Committee of Public Safety, he oversaw the Reign of Terror (1793–94), in which about 17,000 were officially executed, claiming that 'terror is nothing but prompt, severe, inflexible justice'. Fearing they would be next, his colleagues arrested him on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794), and he was guillotined the next day.

Revolutionaries & reformers

Giuseppe Garibaldi

Giuseppe Garibaldi (1807–1882), a sailor from Nice, joined Mazzini's Young Italy movement, was sentenced to death and fled to South America, where he fought in wars in Brazil and Uruguay. Returning, he defended the Roman Republic in 1849. In 1860 he led about 1,000 volunteer 'Redshirts' to conquer Sicily and Naples, then handed them to King Victor Emmanuel II, completing much of Italian unification. Admired worldwide, he inspired Indian nationalists such as Lala Lajpat Rai, who wrote his biography.

Rulers & conquerors

Suleiman the Magnificent

Suleiman I (1494–1566), the tenth Ottoman sultan, reigned for 46 years, the longest of any sultan. He captured Belgrade (1521), Rhodes (1522), defeated Hungary at Mohács (1526), besieged Vienna (1529), and took Baghdad and much of North Africa; his admiral Barbarossa dominated the Mediterranean. Known in Turkey as Kanuni ('the Lawgiver'), he codified laws on taxes and land. He married the former slave Hürrem Sultan (Roxelana), who became very powerful. His architect Mimar Sinan built the Süleymaniye Mosque. He died during a campaign in Hungary.

Rulers & conquerors

Saladin

Salah ad-Din Yusuf ibn Ayyub (1137–1193) was a Kurdish officer who became vizier of Fatimid Egypt in 1169, then abolished the Fatimid caliphate and founded the Ayyubid dynasty. He united Egypt, Syria and parts of Iraq, and on 4 July 1187 destroyed the crusader army at the Horns of Hattin, then took Jerusalem, allowing most Christians to ransom themselves. He fought Richard the Lionheart in the Third Crusade to a truce that kept Jerusalem in Muslim hands. Even European writers praised his chivalry and generosity; he died almost penniless.

Rulers & conquerors

Kublai Khan

Kublai Khan (1215–1294), grandson of Genghis Khan, became Great Khan in 1260 after a civil war with his brother. He moved the capital to Khanbaliq (Dadu, today's Beijing), adopted a Chinese dynastic name, Yuan (1271), and completed the conquest of the Song in 1279, uniting China. He extended the Grand Canal, issued paper money, and welcomed foreigners such as Marco Polo. His invasions of Japan (1274, 1281), Vietnam and Java failed. He patronised Tibetan Buddhism through the lama Phagpa, who designed a script for Mongolian.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Timur

Timur (1336–1405), known in Europe as Tamerlane from 'Timur the Lame' after injuries to his right leg and arm, was a Turco-Mongol from near Samarkand. He conquered Persia, Mesopotamia, the Caucasus and the Golden Horde, sacked Delhi in 1398, and defeated the Ottoman sultan Bayezid I at Ankara (1402). His campaigns, marked by massacres and pyramids of skulls in cities like Isfahan and Baghdad, killed an estimated 17 million people. He brought craftsmen from conquered lands to make Samarkand magnificent, building the Bibi-Khanym mosque. He died while marching to invade China. His descendant Babur founded the Mughal Empire.

Rulers & conquerors

Aurangzeb

Muhiuddin Muhammad Aurangzeb (1618–1707) defeated and executed his brothers, including the scholarly Dara Shikoh, and imprisoned his father Shah Jahan, becoming emperor in 1658. He ruled the Mughal Empire at its largest, conquering Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687). A devout Sunni, he reimposed the jizya on non-Muslims (1679), banned music at court, destroyed some temples including at Mathura and Varanasi, and executed the Sikh Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675). He spent his last 26 years fighting the Marathas in the Deccan, and after his death in 1707 the empire fragmented. Historians debate how much his policies were driven by religion versus politics.

Rulers & conquerors

Humayun

Nasir-ud-din Muhammad Humayun (1508–1556), Babur's son, became emperor in 1530 but faced rebellious brothers and the Afghan Sher Shah Suri, who defeated him at Chausa (1539) and Kannauj (1540). He fled through Sindh, where his son Akbar was born, and took refuge with the Safavid Shah Tahmasp in Persia. With Persian help he recaptured Kabul and then Delhi in 1555, but died in 1556 after falling down the stairs of his library. His tomb in Delhi, built by his widow, inspired the Taj Mahal.

Rulers & conquerors

Sher Shah Suri

Sher Shah Suri (1486–1545), born Farid Khan, was an Afghan who earned the name 'Sher' by killing a tiger. Rising in Bihar, he defeated Humayun and ruled north India from 1540. In five years he reorganised administration, measured land for fair revenue, introduced the silver rupiya (the origin of the rupee), and rebuilt the road from Bengal to Kabul (later the Grand Trunk Road) with sarais and trees. He died in an explosion while besieging Kalinjar fort. His tomb stands in Sasaram.

Rulers & conquerors

Nur Jahan

Mehr-un-Nissa (1577–1645), born to a Persian family in Kandahar, married Emperor Jahangir in 1611 and received the title Nur Jahan ('light of the world'). She effectively ruled alongside him, issuing farmans (orders), having coins struck in her name, and controlling trade. She hunted tigers, designed fabrics and gardens, and built her father's tomb, Itimad-ud-Daulah, in Agra. After Jahangir's death, her son-in-law lost the succession to Shah Jahan, and she retired to Lahore.

Rulers & conquerors

Prithviraj Chauhan

Prithviraj III (about 1166–1192), the Chauhan king of Ajmer and Delhi, fought neighbours including the Chandelas and the Gahadavala king Jaichand of Kannauj; legend says he eloped with Jaichand's daughter Samyukta. In 1191 he defeated Muhammad of Ghor at the first Battle of Tarain, but in 1192 Ghor returned and defeated him; he was captured and killed. The later epic Prithviraj Raso by Chand Bardai romanticised him as the last Hindu emperor of Delhi.

Rulers & conquerors

Alauddin Khalji

Alauddin Khalji (about 1266–1316) raided the rich Yadava capital of Devagiri in 1296, then murdered his uncle Jalaluddin and seized the throne. He repelled several large Mongol invasions, built the fort of Siri, and imposed price controls on grain and goods, with markets supervised by officials and a land tax of half the produce. He conquered Gujarat, Ranthambore and Chittor (1303) — the siege later romanticised in the legend of Rani Padmini — and sent Malik Kafur on raids deep into south India. After his death his empire quickly fell into chaos.

Rulers & conquerors

Muhammad bin Tughlaq

Muhammad bin Tughlaq (about 1290–1351) was a learned, generous but erratic sultan of Delhi. He raised taxes in the Doab during a famine, moved the capital and much of Delhi's population to Daulatabad in the Deccan, then back again, and issued copper token coins that were easily forged, causing chaos. He planned conquests of Khurasan and Qarachil that failed. Ibn Battuta, who served him as a judge, described both his generosity and his cruelty. Revolts during his reign created the Bahmani Sultanate and Vijayanagara.

Rulers & conquerors

Pulakeshin II

Pulakeshin II (reigned about 610–642) of the Chalukyas of Badami seized power from his uncle and expanded his kingdom across the Deccan. Around 618 he defeated Harsha on the Narmada, stopping Harsha's southward expansion. His court poet Ravikirti's Aihole inscription (634) records his victories. Xuanzang visited and praised his warriors. He fought the Pallavas, but in 642 Narasimhavarman I captured Badami and Pulakeshin was probably killed.

Rulers & conquerors

Chandragupta II

Chandragupta II (reigned about 375–415), son of Samudragupta, is identified with the legendary King Vikramaditya. He married Kuberanaga and allied with the Vakatakas by marrying his daughter Prabhavatigupta to their king. He defeated the Western Kshatrapas (Shakas) of Gujarat and Malwa, gaining Arabian Sea ports and earning the title 'Shakari'. The iron pillar at Delhi, which has not rusted for 1,600 years, may commemorate him. The Chinese monk Faxian visited his peaceful empire, and Kalidasa may have been at his court.

Rulers & conquerors

Nebuchadnezzar II

Nebuchadnezzar II (about 634–562 BCE), the greatest king of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, defeated the Egyptians at Carchemish (605 BCE). When Judah rebelled, he captured Jerusalem in 597 BCE and destroyed the city and Solomon's Temple in 587 BCE, deporting its people to Babylon. He rebuilt Babylon with massive walls, the blue-glazed Ishtar Gate (now in Berlin) and the Etemenanki ziggurat, possibly the Tower of Babel; legend credits him with the Hanging Gardens for his Median wife.

Rulers & conquerors

Darius I

Darius I (about 550–486 BCE) seized the Persian throne in 522 BCE after killing a usurper, recording his victory in the trilingual Behistun inscription, key to deciphering cuneiform. He divided the empire into about 20 satrapies, standardised taxes and the gold daric coin, built the Royal Road and a canal from the Nile to the Red Sea, and began Persepolis. He conquered the Indus valley, which paid the largest tribute. He crushed the Ionian Revolt, but his army was defeated at Marathon (490 BCE).

Rulers & conquerors

Tutankhamun

Tutankhamun (about 1341–1323 BCE) became pharaoh at about nine; he was probably the son of Akhenaten. Under his advisers Ay and Horemheb, he restored the worship of Amun and the old gods and moved the court back from Amarna. He died at about 19, possibly from an infection after a leg fracture and malaria. His small tomb in the Valley of the Kings, discovered almost intact by Howard Carter in 1922, contained over 5,000 objects, including the golden death mask, making him the most famous pharaoh today.

Rulers & conquerors

Constantine the Great

Constantine I (272–337) was proclaimed emperor by troops at York in 306. Before the Battle of the Milvian Bridge (312) he reportedly saw a cross with the words 'In this sign conquer'; after victory he favoured Christianity, and with Licinius issued the Edict of Milan (313) granting religious freedom. He called the Council of Nicaea (325) to settle the Arian controversy, became sole emperor in 324, and founded Constantinople in 330 as the new capital. He introduced the gold solidus and was baptised on his deathbed.

Rulers & conquerors

Marcus Aurelius

Marcus Aurelius (121–180), the last of Rome's 'Five Good Emperors', ruled from 161. His reign saw the Antonine Plague and long wars against the Marcomanni and other Germanic tribes on the Danube frontier. In his tent he wrote the Meditations, private notes in Greek on Stoic philosophy, urging self-discipline, duty and acceptance of fate. He made his son Commodus heir, ending the era of adoptive succession, and Commodus's misrule followed.

Rulers & conquerors

Catherine the Great

Catherine II (1729–1796) was a German princess, Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst, who married the future Peter III of Russia. In 1762 she overthrew her husband in a palace coup (he was killed soon after) and ruled for 34 years. She expanded Russia by annexing Crimea, defeating the Ottomans, and partitioning Poland. She corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot, founded the Hermitage collection and the Smolny Institute for girls, but strengthened serfdom and crushed Pugachev's rebellion (1773–75).

Rulers & conquerors

Louis XIV

Louis XIV (1638–1715) became king at four; his childhood was marked by the Fronde revolts, which made him determined to control the nobility. Ruling personally from 1661, he declared himself the 'Sun King', moved the court to the vast palace of Versailles, where nobles competed for his favour, and centralised power with ministers like Colbert, who promoted industry and colonies, including the French East India Company (1664) and Pondicherry. He fought long wars for French expansion, revoked the Edict of Nantes (1685), driving out Protestant Huguenots, and left France deeply in debt. His 72-year reign is the longest of any European monarch.

Rulers & conquerors

Henry VIII

Henry VIII (1491–1547) became king of England in 1509. Desperate for a male heir, he sought to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon; when the Pope refused, he broke with Rome, making himself Supreme Head of the Church of England (1534) and dissolving the monasteries, seizing their wealth. He married six times: two wives were divorced, two (Anne Boleyn and Catherine Howard) beheaded, one died after childbirth, and one survived him. He executed advisers like Thomas More and Thomas Cromwell, built up the navy, and his children Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I all reigned.

Rulers & conquerors

Isabella I of Castile

Isabella I (1451–1504) became queen of Castile in 1474 after a succession war; her marriage to Ferdinand of Aragon united Spain's two largest kingdoms. Together, the 'Catholic Monarchs' established the Spanish Inquisition (1478), conquered Granada (1492), ending Muslim rule in Iberia, and in the same year expelled Jews who refused to convert. Isabella sponsored Columbus's voyage. Her daughter Catherine of Aragon married Henry VIII, and her grandson became Emperor Charles V.

Rulers & conquerors

Charles V

Charles V (1500–1558), grandson of Ferdinand and Isabella and of Emperor Maximilian, inherited the Netherlands, Spain with its American empire, Austria and southern Italy, and was elected Holy Roman Emperor in 1519 — ruling an empire 'on which the sun never set'. He faced Luther at the Diet of Worms (1521), wars with France, Ottoman expansion under Suleiman, and the conquests of the Aztecs and Incas by his conquistadors. Exhausted, he abdicated in 1556, dividing his lands between his son Philip II (Spain) and brother Ferdinand (Austria), and retired to a monastery.

Rulers & conquerors

Moctezuma II

Moctezuma Xocoyotzin (about 1466–1520) became huey tlatoani of the Aztec Empire in 1502, expanding it to its greatest extent and centralising power. When Hernán Cortés landed in 1519, Moctezuma sent gifts and received him in Tenochtitlan, perhaps due to diplomacy or uncertainty. Cortés took him hostage. After Spanish massacres during a festival led to revolt, Moctezuma was killed in 1520 — Spanish accounts say by stones thrown by his own people, Aztec accounts say by the Spanish.

Rulers & conquerors

Pachacuti

Pachacuti ('he who shakes the earth', 1418–1471) was the son of the ruler of Cusco. When the Chanka people attacked the city, his father fled, but Pachacuti defended it and won a legendary victory. He became Sapa Inca in 1438 and transformed the kingdom into the empire of Tawantinsuyu, conquering the Andes from Lake Titicaca to central Peru. He rebuilt Cusco in stone, reorganised administration, roads and the cult of Inti, and probably built Machu Picchu as a royal estate.

Rulers & conquerors

Atahualpa

Atahualpa (about 1502–1533), son of Huayna Capac, fought a bloody civil war with his half-brother Huáscar after their father died of smallpox, and won in 1532. At Cajamarca, Francisco Pizarro's 168 Spaniards ambushed his unarmed retinue and captured him. He offered to fill a room with gold and two with silver for his release; Pizarro took the ransom but executed him in July 1533 on charges including the murder of Huáscar.

Rulers & conquerors

Shaka Zulu

Shaka (about 1787–1828), the illegitimate son of a Zulu chief, grew up in exile and served in the Mthethwa army under Dingiswayo. After becoming Zulu chief in 1816, he armed warriors with the short stabbing spear and large cowhide shields, organised age regiments and used the 'bull horn' formation to encircle enemies. He built a kingdom that dominated southeast Africa during the mfecane, a period of wars and migrations. He became increasingly brutal, especially after his mother's death, and was assassinated by his half-brothers in 1828.

Rulers & conquerors

Haile Selassie

Haile Selassie (1892–1975), born Tafari Makonnen, became regent of Ethiopia in 1916 and emperor in 1930, claiming descent from Solomon. He introduced a constitution and modernisation. When Mussolini invaded in 1935, using poison gas, he appealed to the League of Nations, warning 'it is us today, it will be you tomorrow'. He lived in exile in Britain and returned with British help in 1941. He helped found the Organisation of African Unity (1963). Rastafarians revere him as divine. Famine and unrest led to his deposition by the Derg in 1974; he died in custody.

Freedom fighters & leaders

Kwame Nkrumah

Kwame Nkrumah (1909–1972) studied in the US and Britain, where he joined Pan-African circles. Returning to the Gold Coast, he founded the Convention People's Party, led strikes and 'positive action' inspired by Gandhi, and was jailed. After election victories he became prime minister, and Ghana became the first sub-Saharan colony to gain independence on 6 March 1957. He championed African unity and the Non-Aligned Movement, but became authoritarian, declaring a one-party state. He was overthrown in a 1966 coup while visiting China.

Rulers & conquerors

Indira Gandhi

Indira Gandhi (1917–1984), Nehru's only child, became India's first woman Prime Minister in 1966. She nationalised banks, abolished princely privy purses and led India to victory in the 1971 war that created Bangladesh, followed by the first nuclear test (1974). After a court invalidated her election, she declared a state of Emergency (1975–77), suspending civil liberties; she lost the 1977 election but returned in 1980. She ordered Operation Blue Star (1984) against militants in the Golden Temple, and was assassinated by two Sikh bodyguards on 31 October 1984, followed by anti-Sikh riots.

Rulers & conquerors

Lal Bahadur Shastri

Lal Bahadur Shastri (1904–1966), from a modest family in Mughalsarai, joined the freedom struggle and spent about nine years in jail. As railway minister he resigned taking moral responsibility for a train accident. He became Prime Minister in 1964 after Nehru, led India in the 1965 war with Pakistan, and coined the slogan 'Jai Jawan Jai Kisan', promoting the Green Revolution and White Revolution. He died suddenly in Tashkent in January 1966, a day after signing a peace agreement; his death has been the subject of persistent conspiracy theories, though officially it was a heart attack.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Vlad the Impaler

Vlad III Dracula (1431–1476), prince of Wallachia (in today's Romania), was held hostage by the Ottomans as a boy. His surname means 'son of the dragon' after his father's Order of the Dragon. During his rule he killed thousands of boyars, criminals and Ottoman soldiers by impalement; in 1462 the invading Sultan Mehmed II reportedly found a 'forest' of 20,000 impaled bodies. He is a national hero in Romania for resisting the Ottomans, and Bram Stoker borrowed his name for Dracula (1897).

Tyrants & notorious figures

Caligula

Gaius Caesar 'Caligula' (12–41), whose nickname means 'little boot' from the soldier's boots he wore as a child, became emperor in 37 to great popularity. After a severe illness, ancient historians describe him as cruel and erratic: executing rivals, spending the treasury on luxuries, claiming divinity and supposedly planning to make his horse a consul (likely a joke). Modern historians caution that sources were hostile. He was assassinated by his Praetorian Guard in 41.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Nero

Nero (37–68) became emperor at 16 thanks to his mother Agrippina, who probably poisoned his predecessor Claudius; he later had her murdered. His reign began well under his tutor Seneca, but turned to extravagance and cruelty. After the Great Fire of Rome (64), he blamed Christians, many of whom were burned alive, and built the lavish Golden House. Revolts led the Senate to declare him a public enemy, and he took his own life in 68.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Ivan the Terrible

Ivan IV (1530–1584) became Grand Prince of Moscow at three and the first crowned Tsar of Russia in 1547. He conquered the khanates of Kazan and Astrakhan, began expansion into Siberia, and built St Basil's Cathedral. After his wife's death he became increasingly paranoid, creating the oprichnina, a state within the state, whose black-clad horsemen terrorised the nobility and massacred Novgorod (1570). In 1581 he killed his own son in a rage.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Leopold II of Belgium

Leopold II (1835–1909), King of the Belgians, used a supposed humanitarian association to acquire the Congo as his personal property, recognised at the Berlin Conference (1885). His Congo Free State forced Congolese to collect rubber and ivory; quotas were enforced by hostage-taking, burning villages and cutting off hands. Millions died from violence, famine and disease. Exposés by Edmund Morel, Roger Casement and Mark Twain forced Belgium to take over the colony in 1908.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Benito Mussolini

Benito Mussolini (1883–1945), a former socialist journalist, founded the Fascist movement in 1919. After the 'March on Rome' (1922), he became prime minister and built a one-party dictatorship. He invaded Ethiopia (1935–36), using poison gas, allied with Hitler, and passed anti-Jewish racial laws (1938). Italy's defeats led to his removal in 1943. Rescued by German commandos, he led a puppet state until partisans shot him in April 1945.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Pol Pot

Pol Pot (born Saloth Sar, 1925–1998) studied in Paris, joined the Communist Party, and led the Khmer Rouge to victory in Cambodia in 1975. His regime emptied cities, abolished money, schools and religion, and forced people into agricultural communes in an attempt to create an agrarian utopia ('Year Zero'). About 1.5–2 million people — a quarter of the population — died from execution, starvation and overwork in the 'killing fields'. Vietnam invaded in 1979; he died in the jungle.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Idi Amin

Idi Amin (about 1925–2003), a former boxer who served in Britain's colonial army, seized power in Uganda in a 1971 coup. In 1972 he expelled about 80,000 Asians, mostly of Indian origin, giving them 90 days to leave and seizing their businesses, which wrecked the economy. His regime killed perhaps 100,000–500,000 people. His invasion of Tanzania led to his overthrow in 1979; he lived in exile in Saudi Arabia.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Reginald Dyer

Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer (1864–1927), born in Murree, commanded troops in Amritsar in April 1919. On 13 April, Baisakhi, he marched soldiers into Jallianwala Bagh and ordered them to fire without warning on a peaceful gathering, killing at least 379 people (Indian estimates exceed 1,000), and later imposed a 'crawling order'. The Hunter Committee criticised him and he was forced to retire, but British supporters raised money for him as a hero.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Robert Clive

Robert Clive (1725–1774) went to Madras as a clerk of the East India Company, became a soldier, and won fame defending Arcot (1751). At Plassey (1757) he defeated Siraj ud-Daulah by bribing Mir Jafar, gaining huge personal wealth. As Governor of Bengal he obtained the diwani (1765). Accused of corruption in Parliament, he said he was 'astonished at my own moderation'. He died in 1774, probably by suicide.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Alexander Graham Bell

Born in Edinburgh to a family of speech teachers — his mother and wife were deaf — Bell emigrated to Canada and then Boston, where he taught deaf students. Experimenting with transmitting multiple telegraph messages, he and Thomas Watson developed the telephone, patented on 7 March 1876, hours after his lawyer beat Elisha Gray's filing; the first call followed on 10 March. The Bell Telephone Company grew into AT&T. Bell later worked on the photophone, hydrofoils and aircraft, and co-founded the National Geographic Society. His support for eugenic ideas about deaf people and for suppressing sign language is now criticised.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Antonio Meucci

Meucci, a Florentine stage technician, emigrated to Cuba and then Staten Island, New York, where he developed a 'telettrofono' to talk between his workshop and his paralysed wife's bedroom. Poor and speaking little English, he filed a patent caveat in 1871 but could not afford to renew it after 1874. When Bell patented the telephone, Meucci sued; the case ended with his death. In 2002 the US House of Representatives recognised his contribution.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Wright brothers

Wilbur and Orville Wright ran a bicycle shop and never finished high school. Inspired by glider pioneer Otto Lilienthal's death, they studied flight control, built a wind tunnel and invented wing-warping. On 17 December 1903 at Kitty Hawk they made four flights, the longest 260 m. They spent years in patent battles with Glenn Curtiss, which slowed American aviation; Wilbur died of typhoid in 1912.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Guglielmo Marconi

Marconi, from a wealthy family in Bologna, built on Hertz's work to develop long-distance wireless telegraphy, moving to Britain in 1896 to patent it. He claimed to receive the first transatlantic signal in 1901 and shared the 1909 Nobel Prize. His company's operators on the Titanic sent distress calls in 1912. In 1943 the US Supreme Court upheld earlier patents of Tesla and others. He later joined Mussolini's Fascist party.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

J. Robert Oppenheimer

Oppenheimer, a brilliant theoretical physicist who read the Bhagavad Gita in Sanskrit, led the Los Alamos laboratory of the Manhattan Project from 1943. After the Trinity test he recalled the line 'Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds'. After the war he chaired the Atomic Energy Commission's advisory committee and opposed the hydrogen bomb. In 1954, amid McCarthyism, a hearing revoked his security clearance over past communist associations; the US Department of Energy vacated that decision in 2022.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Enrico Fermi

Fermi won the 1938 Nobel Prize for work on neutron-induced radioactivity and used the trip to Stockholm to flee Fascist Italy, as his wife was Jewish. In Chicago he led the team that built Chicago Pile-1, achieving the first controlled nuclear chain reaction on 2 December 1942, and worked on the Manhattan Project.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Lise Meitner

Meitner, one of the first women to earn a physics doctorate in Vienna, worked with Otto Hahn in Berlin for 30 years. As a Jew she fled Nazi Germany in 1938. With her nephew Otto Frisch she explained Hahn's results as nuclear fission. Hahn alone received the 1944 Nobel Prize. She refused to work on the atomic bomb. Element 109, meitnerium, honours her.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Alexander Fleming

Fleming served as a doctor in WWI, seeing soldiers die of infected wounds. In 1928 at St Mary's Hospital he noticed Penicillium mould killing bacteria. He shared the 1945 Nobel Prize with Florey and Chain, who turned it into a drug, and warned about antibiotic resistance.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Johannes Gutenberg

Gutenberg, a goldsmith, developed the European movable-type printing system around 1440–1450 and printed the Gutenberg Bible. He lost his workshop to his financier Johann Fust in a lawsuit in 1455 and died with little recognition.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

James Watt

Watt, an instrument maker at Glasgow University, added a separate condenser to the steam engine (1765) and, with Matthew Boulton, built rotary engines that powered factories. The unit of power, the watt, honours him.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Edward Jenner

Jenner tested cowpox inoculation on James Phipps in 1796 and promoted vaccination worldwide, sending vaccine to India by 1802. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Tim Berners-Lee

Berners-Lee proposed the World Wide Web at CERN in 1989, wrote HTML, HTTP and the first browser, and gave it to the world royalty-free. He founded the W3C and campaigns for an open web and data privacy.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Rosalind Franklin

Franklin's X-ray diffraction work at King's College London, especially Photo 51, was key to the double-helix model, shown to Watson without her knowledge. She also studied viruses and coal. She died of ovarian cancer at 37, four years before the Nobel Prize she could not share.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Wernher von Braun

Von Braun joined the Nazi Party and SS and led development of the V-2 missile, built by concentration camp prisoners. Brought to the US under Operation Paperclip, he led NASA's Saturn V rocket that took Apollo astronauts to the Moon.

Tyrants & notorious figures

Henry Ford

Ford's moving assembly line (1913) made the Model T affordable and doubled workers' wages to $5 a day. He also published antisemitic propaganda, including the forged Protocols of the Elders of Zion, praised by Hitler.

Thinkers, scientists & artists

Steve Jobs

Jobs co-founded Apple in 1976, was ousted in 1985, returned in 1997, and launched the iPod, iPhone and iPad. He visited India in 1974 seeking spiritual guidance.
