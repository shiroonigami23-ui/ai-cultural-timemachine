Human origins

New fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco and the pan-African origin of Homo sapiens

Re-dating of fossils from Jebel Irhoud, Morocco, using thermoluminescence on burnt flints gave an age of about 315,000 years, pushing back the origin of Homo sapiens by about 100,000 years. The skulls have modern faces but elongated braincases, suggesting the modern face evolved first and the round brain later. Because the site is in North Africa, far from East Africa, the authors argued our species emerged across the whole continent rather than in a single 'Garden of Eden'.

Human origins

A draft sequence of the Neandertal genome

Svante Pääbo's team sequenced about 4 billion base pairs of Neanderthal DNA from bones in Croatia's Vindija Cave. Comparing it with modern genomes, they found that non-Africans carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA (later refined to about 2%), proving interbreeding. The work helped earn Pääbo the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine and launched the field of palaeogenomics.

Human migration

Human occupation of northern Australia by 65,000 years ago

Excavations at the Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land found ground-edge axes, grinding stones and ochre in layers dated by optically stimulated luminescence to about 65,000 years ago. If correct, people reached Australia earlier than thought, implying an earlier departure from Africa; some researchers argue the dates are too old.

Prehistoric India

Early Pleistocene presence of Acheulian hominins in South India

Cosmogenic nuclide dating at Attirampakkam near Chennai, Tamil Nadu, showed Acheulean hand-axes and cleavers about 1.5 million years old, among the oldest outside Africa. This showed that hand-axe-making hominins, probably Homo erectus, reached India very early.

Prehistoric India

Middle Paleolithic assemblages from the Indian subcontinent before and after the Toba super-eruption

At Jwalapuram in Andhra Pradesh, stone tools were found both below and above the 74,000-year-old Toba ash layer, with similar styles. The authors argued that humans in India survived the eruption, challenging the idea of a global volcanic winter that nearly wiped out humanity.

Ancient DNA

The formation of human populations in South and Central Asia

Analysing 523 ancient genomes, this study showed that South Asians descend mainly from a mixture of Indus Valley-related people (themselves a mix of Iranian-related farmers or hunter-gatherers and South Asian hunter-gatherers) and steppe pastoralists who arrived around 2000–1500 BCE. It supported the arrival of Indo-European languages from the steppe.

Indus civilisation

An ancient Harappan genome lacks ancestry from steppe pastoralists or Iranian farmers

The first genome from an Indus Valley person, a woman buried at Rakhigarhi in Haryana about 4,500 years ago, showed ancestry from South Asian hunter-gatherers and an early Iranian-related lineage that split before farming, with no steppe ancestry. This suggests farming in South Asia developed locally rather than being brought by West Asian farmers.

Indus civilisation

Fluvial landscapes of the Harappan civilization

Using satellite data and sediment cores, the authors showed that the Harappan heartland relied on monsoon-fed rivers rather than a Himalayan-fed Sarasvati, and that a weakening monsoon after about 2000 BCE caused rivers to dry, leading people to move east toward the Ganga plain.

Indus civilisation

Oxygen isotope in archaeological bioapatites from India: implications to climate change and decline of Bronze Age Harappan civilization

Oxygen isotopes in animal bones and teeth from Bhirrana, Haryana, showed a weakening monsoon around 7,000–5,000 years ago and drought linked to the 4.2-kiloyear event. Harappans adapted by shifting crops but ultimately abandoned large cities.

Climate and collapse

What drives societal collapse?

This review in Science examined collapses of societies from the Maya to the Bronze Age, arguing that climate shocks combine with social vulnerabilities — inequality, rigid institutions and dependence on networks — to cause collapse, and that resilience depends on adaptability.

Ancient DNA

Massive migration from the steppe was a source for Indo-European languages in Europe

Genomes from 69 ancient Europeans showed that around 4,500 years ago, Yamnaya-related steppe herders replaced about 75% of the ancestry of Central Europe's Corded Ware people. This supported the steppe origin of Indo-European languages.

Climate and collapse

Development and disintegration of Maya political systems in response to climate change

A 2,000-year rainfall record from Yok Balum cave in Belize showed that wet periods coincided with Maya population growth and political expansion, while a long drying trend from 660 to 1000 CE correlated with warfare and the collapse of kingdoms.

Climate and collapse

Cooling and societal change during the Late Antique Little Ice Age from 536 to around 660 AD

Tree rings from the Alps and Altai showed a cold period after eruptions in 536, 540 and 547, linking it to the Plague of Justinian, the fall of the Sasanians, Slavic migrations and the rise of the Arab empire.

Pandemics

A draft genome of Yersinia pestis from victims of the Black Death

DNA from teeth of victims buried in London's East Smithfield plague pit (1348–1350) reconstructed the genome of the Black Death bacterium, showing it was ancestral to all modern plague strains and not fundamentally more virulent.

Ancient DNA

Reconstructing Indian population history

Genotyping 132 people from 25 Indian groups, this study showed that most Indians descend from a mixture of two ancestral populations: 'Ancestral North Indians' related to West Eurasians and 'Ancestral South Indians' found nowhere else. The mixture varies by region and caste, and the paper showed that caste groups have been largely endogamous for thousands of years. It set the framework for later ancient-DNA studies of South Asia.

Ancient DNA

Genetic history of an archaic hominin group from Denisova Cave in Siberia

Sequencing the genome from a finger bone found in Denisova Cave revealed a previously unknown human group, the Denisovans, sister to Neanderthals. Melanesians (such as Papua New Guineans) carry about 4–6% Denisovan DNA, showing interbreeding with modern humans in Asia. It was the first time a human group was identified from DNA alone.

Ancient DNA

The complete mitochondrial DNA genome of an unknown hominin from southern Siberia

This paper first reported that mitochondrial DNA from the Denisova finger bone was distinct from both Neanderthals and modern humans, diverging about a million years ago. It triggered the discovery of the Denisovans.

Ancient DNA

A complete genome of a Neanderthal from the Altai Mountains

A high-quality genome from a Neanderthal woman's toe bone showed her parents were closely related (likely half-siblings), and revealed gene flow among Neanderthals, Denisovans, modern humans and an unknown 'superarchaic' population.

Ancient DNA

Altitude adaptation in Tibetans caused by introgression of Denisovan-like DNA

The EPAS1 gene variant that helps Tibetans live at high altitude without excessive red blood cells was inherited from Denisovans — a striking example of archaic genes helping modern humans adapt.

Human origins

A late Middle Pleistocene Denisovan mandible from the Tibetan Plateau

A jawbone found by a monk in Baishiya Karst Cave in Xiahe, China, dated to at least 160,000 years ago, was identified as Denisovan using ancient proteins — the first Denisovan fossil outside Siberia, showing they lived at high altitude long before modern humans.

Human origins

Late Middle Pleistocene Harbin cranium represents a new Homo species

Described the 146,000-year-old 'Dragon Man' skull from Harbin, with a large braincase and big molars, naming it Homo longi and arguing it was closer to Homo sapiens than Neanderthals were. Later protein analysis linked it to Denisovans.

Human origins

A new small-bodied hominin from the Late Pleistocene of Flores, Indonesia

The description of Homo floresiensis, the 'Hobbit', a one-metre-tall human with a brain the size of a chimpanzee's from Liang Bua cave. It showed that small-brained humans survived until recently and sparked debate over island dwarfism.

Human origins

Homo naledi, a new species of the genus Homo from the Dinaledi Chamber, South Africa

Over 1,500 fossils from at least 15 individuals found deep in South Africa's Rising Star cave described a new species with a mix of primitive and modern features. Later dating showed it lived about 335,000–236,000 years ago, overlapping with early Homo sapiens.

Human origins

3.3-million-year-old stone tools from Lomekwi 3, West Turkana, Kenya

Reported the oldest known stone tools, 700,000 years older than the Oldowan and older than the genus Homo, suggesting that australopithecines or Kenyanthropus made tools.

Human origins

Microstratigraphic evidence of in situ fire in the Acheulean strata of Wonderwerk Cave

Microscopic analysis of burned bone and plant ash about 30 m inside Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa, showed hominins used fire about 1 million years ago — the earliest secure evidence, supporting the 'cooking hypothesis' of human evolution.

Human origins

Lower Palaeolithic hunting spears from Germany

The description of wooden throwing spears from Schöningen, about 300,000 years old, found with butchered horses, proving that pre-Neanderthal humans were organised big-game hunters.

Human origins

Narrative cave art in Indonesia by 51,200 years ago

Laser-based uranium-series dating of a painted scene of a wild pig and human-like figures in Leang Karampuang cave, Sulawesi, gave a minimum age of 51,200 years — the oldest known figurative and narrative art.

Human migration

Evidence of humans in North America during the Last Glacial Maximum

Seeds in sediment above and below human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, dated them to 23,000–21,000 years ago, challenging the idea that people arrived only after the ice sheets melted.

Ancient DNA

Population genomics of Bronze Age Eurasia

Genomes of 101 ancient Eurasians showed large-scale Bronze Age migrations from the steppe into Europe and Central Asia and found that lactose tolerance was still rare then.

Ancient DNA

Genomic insights into the origin of farming in the ancient Near East

Showed that the first farmers of Anatolia, the Levant and Iran were genetically distinct; Iranian farmer-related ancestry spread east toward South Asia, while Anatolian farmers populated Europe.

Ancient DNA

The origins and spread of domestic horses from the Western Eurasian steppes

By sequencing 273 ancient horses, this study found that modern domestic horses originated in the lower Volga–Don region about 2200 BCE and spread across Eurasia within centuries with chariots.

Human migration

Dispersals and genetic adaptation of Bantu-speaking populations in Africa and North America

Genomic data from 2,000 Africans traced the Bantu expansion from West-Central Africa through the rainforest and the Great Lakes, and showed genes for immunity adapted after mixing with local peoples.

Human migration

Paths and timings of the peopling of Polynesia inferred from genomic networks

Genetic data from 430 Pacific islanders reconstructed the sequence of voyages from Samoa to the Cook Islands, the Society Islands and then remote islands such as Rapa Nui, mostly between 830 and 1360 CE.

Pandemics

Early divergent strains of Yersinia pestis in Eurasia 5,000 years ago

Plague DNA in Bronze Age teeth showed Yersinia pestis infected humans 3,000 years earlier than written records, though early strains lacked the gene for flea transmission.

Pandemics

Yersinia pestis and the Plague of Justinian 541–543 AD: a genomic analysis

DNA from victims in Bavaria proved the Plague of Justinian was caused by Yersinia pestis, a lineage independent from the later Black Death strain that died out.

Climate and collapse

Climate and the collapse of Maya civilization

Titanium levels in sediments of the Cariaco Basin revealed multi-year droughts around 810, 860 and 910 CE, matching the stages of collapse of Classic Maya cities.

Climate and collapse

Timing and climate forcing of volcanic eruptions for the past 2,500 years

Ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica produced a precise volcanic record, showing that 15 of the 16 coldest summers since 500 BCE followed large eruptions, including 536 CE.

Climate and collapse

Extreme climate after massive eruption of Alaska's Okmok volcano in 43 BCE and effects on the late Roman Republic and Ptolemaic Kingdom

Linked an Alaskan eruption to two of the coldest years in the Northern Hemisphere, failed Nile floods, and famine — conditions during the turmoil after Julius Caesar's death and before the end of Cleopatra's Egypt.
