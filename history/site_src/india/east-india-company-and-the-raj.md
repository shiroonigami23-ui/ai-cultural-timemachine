On **31 December 1600**, Queen Elizabeth I granted a royal charter to "the Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies". Within 160 years this private company had its own army and was collecting the taxes of Bengal, one of the richest regions on Earth.

## From traders to rulers

:::collapse !Key steps
- **1608–1615:** the Company trades at **Surat**; Sir Thomas Roe wins trading rights from Mughal emperor Jahangir.
- **1639–1690:** forts and factories at **Madras** (Fort St George), **Bombay** (from Portugal, 1668) and **Calcutta** (1690).
- **1746–1763:** **Carnatic Wars** against the French in the south.
- **1757:** **Battle of Plassey**. Robert Clive defeats Nawab Siraj ud-Daulah, mainly thanks to Mir Jafar's betrayal.
- **1764–65:** after **Buxar**, the Treaty of Allahabad gives the Company the ***diwani***, the right to collect revenue in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
- **1767–1799:** **Anglo-Mysore Wars**; **Tipu Sultan** dies at Srirangapatna (1799).
- **1775–1818:** **Anglo-Maratha Wars** end Maratha power.
- **1845–1849:** **Anglo-Sikh Wars**; Punjab annexed and the Koh-i-Noor taken.
- **1848–1856:** Lord Dalhousie's **Doctrine of Lapse** annexes Satara, Jhansi, Nagpur; Awadh is annexed in 1856.
:::

By 1803 the Company's armies numbered about **260,000**, around twice the size of the British Army.

## Revenue, trade and famine

The Company used Bengal's revenues to buy Indian goods for export, so Britain gained Indian textiles without paying for them, part of what Dadabhai Naoroji later called the **"drain of wealth"**. The **Permanent Settlement** (1793) fixed land tax through zamindars; the **ryotwari** system in Madras and Bombay taxed farmers directly. In the **Bengal famine of 1770**, about a third of Bengal's people died, while revenue collection continued. Indian **textile exports collapsed** as British machine-made cloth flooded India after 1813. The Company also grew **opium** in Bihar and Malwa and forced it on China, leading to the **Opium Wars**.

## 1857: the great uprising

In May 1857 sepoys at **Meerut** mutinied, angered by cartridges said to be greased with cow and pig fat, by annexations and by deep economic grievances. They marched to Delhi and proclaimed **Bahadur Shah Zafar** emperor. Leaders included **Rani Lakshmibai** of Jhansi, **Nana Sahib**, **Tantia Tope**, **Begum Hazrat Mahal** of Lucknow and **Kunwar Singh**. The British crushed the rising with great brutality. In **1858** the Company was abolished and India passed to the **Crown**.

## The Raj (1858–1947)

- **Government:** a Viceroy ruled British India directly, while ~560 princely states were ruled indirectly.
- **Infrastructure:** about 67,000 km of railway by 1947, canals in Punjab, telegraphs, ports; designed mainly for exports and military control.
- **Famines:** the famines of **1876–78**, **1896–97** and **1899–1900** killed perhaps 12–29 million people in total as grain kept moving to markets and Britain. The **1943 Bengal famine** killed about 2–3 million.
- **Economy:** India's share of world GDP fell from roughly 16% in 1820 to about 4% by 1950 (Maddison); life expectancy in the 1920s was around 25 years.
- **Society:** the census fixed caste categories; English education created a new middle class that would lead the national movement.

## Debate

Some historians emphasise lasting legacies such as railways, a unified administration, English and parliamentary institutions; others stress extraction, deindustrialisation and famine. Most agree the Indian economy barely grew per head during two centuries of British rule.
