title: The Industrial Revolution
summary: How coal, steam and machines transformed Britain and then the world after 1760, what factory life was like, and why it deindustrialised India.

---
Between about **1760 and 1840** Britain shifted from hand production to **machines powered by water, then steam and coal**. It was the biggest change in human life since farming.

## Key inventions

- **Textiles:** the flying shuttle (1733), spinning jenny (1764), water frame (1769), spinning mule (1779), power loom (1785).
- **Power:** Newcomen's steam engine (1712), improved by **James Watt** (1769–76).
- **Iron and coal:** coke smelting; canals and then **railways** (Stephenson's Rocket, 1829).
- **Communication:** telegraph (1830s–40s).

## Why Britain?

Historians point to cheap **coal**, expensive **labour** (which made machines worth buying), **colonial markets and raw cotton**, banks and patents, and a culture of practical science. A key driver was the effort to copy **Indian cotton cloth** (calico, chintz, muslin), which British consumers loved; British laws even banned Indian printed calicoes (1700, 1721) to protect domestic producers.

## Life in the factories

People moved from villages to crowded towns such as **Manchester**. Children as young as five worked 12–16 hour days in mills and mines until Factory Acts slowly limited hours. Cholera and typhoid spread in slums. Over the long run, though, wages and life expectancy rose and child mortality fell.

## India deindustrialised

India had been the world's largest textile producer. After 1813 British machine-made cloth entered India with low tariffs while Indian cloth faced high duties in Britain. Weavers of Dhaka and Bengal were ruined; Dhaka's population fell sharply. India's share of world manufacturing output fell from about **25% (1750) to about 2% (1900)** (Bairoch's estimates). The first Indian cotton mills opened in **Bombay** in the 1850s, and the **Tata** steel plant at Jamshedpur in 1907.

## The long legacy

Industrialisation began the rise in atmospheric CO₂ (from ~280 ppm to over 420 ppm today), and with it modern climate change.
