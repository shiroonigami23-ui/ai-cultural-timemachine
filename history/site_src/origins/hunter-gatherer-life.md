For about **95% of the time *Homo sapiens* has existed**, everyone lived by hunting, fishing and gathering. Some peoples, such as the **San** of the Kalahari, the **Hadza** of Tanzania, the **Jarawa** and **Sentinelese** of the Andamans and Arctic **Inuit**, kept these ways of life into recent times.

## Daily life

:::collapse !Bands, camps and moving with the seasons
- **Group size:** bands of roughly **25–50 people**, linked by marriage and friendship into wider networks of hundreds.
- **Mobility:** camps moved every few weeks or seasons, following ripening plants, migrating animals and water. Some rich coasts and river valleys allowed semi-settled villages.
- **Homes:** caves and rock shelters (Bhimbetka in India), brush huts, hide tents, and mammoth-bone houses on the Ice Age Ukrainian plain.
- **Division of work:** often men hunted large game and women gathered plants and small animals, but practices varied and many women hunted; children foraged too.
- **Sharing:** meat was shared widely; stinginess was mocked. Many such societies were strikingly **egalitarian**, levelling bossiness with jokes and gossip.
:::

## What they ate

Hundreds of plants, nuts, roots, honey, fish, shellfish, insects and game. At Ohalo II (Israel, 23,000 years ago) people collected wild barley and wheat. Diets were varied, but famine and seasonal hunger were real risks.

## Tools and knowledge

Hand-axes gave way to **blades, microliths, spear-throwers (atlatls), bows and arrows (~70,000 years ago in Africa), harpoons, nets, fish-hooks, needles**, baskets and boats. Hunter-gatherers held encyclopaedic knowledge of animal behaviour, plant medicines and poisons, star navigation and tracking.

## Art, music and belief

Cave paintings (Lascaux, Chauvet, Bhimbetka, Sulawesi), carved figurines, bone flutes (~40,000 years ago), body ornaments and burials with grave goods show rich symbolic lives. Many groups practised **animism** (seeing spirits in animals and places) and **shamanic** trance dances, as the San still do.

## Health

Life expectancy at birth was low (~30) because many children died, but adults who survived often reached 60 or 70. Hunter-gatherers had fewer crowd diseases and dental cavities than early farmers.

## The end of an era

Farming spread because it fed more people per square kilometre, not because it made life easier. Farmers out-reproduced foragers and pushed them into deserts, forests and the Arctic. Today, the remaining hunter-gatherers face land loss, and some, like the Sentinelese, choose isolation.
